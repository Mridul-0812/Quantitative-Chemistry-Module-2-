5. What is a Mole?

The Giant "Pile" of Tiny Things

It's Just a Number

If you go to a bakery and ask for a dozen donuts, you get 12. If you ask for a pair of socks, you get 2. If you ask for a gross of pencils, you get 144.

The mole is exactly like a dozen, but it's used by chemists because atoms are so incredibly small that counting them in "dozens" would be like trying to measure the distance to the moon in millimetres.

Definition: A mole is the amount of substance that contains exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, or ions).

This massive number is known as Avogadro's Constant, denoted as $N_A$.

The Mind-Blowing Scale of $N_A$

The number $602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000$ is hard to wrap your head around. To help visualise "The Pile," let's look at what a mole of "everyday" items would look like:

Basketballs

A mole of basketballs would create a new planet the size of the Earth.

Marshmallows

A mole of marshmallows would cover the USA in a layer 100km deep.

Water Molecules

A mole of water fits into roughly 18ml (about one tablespoon).

Why $6.022 \times 10^{23}$?

Why this specific, weird number? It was originally defined as the number of atoms in exactly 12 grams of Carbon-12. By using this number, we create a bridge between the atomic world (Atomic Mass Units) and the human world (Grams).

If one atom of Carbon weighs 12 amu, then 1 mole of Carbon atoms weighs exactly 12 grams.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Basic Counting

How many atoms of Hydrogen are in 1 mole of Methane ($CH_4$)?

Step 1: Analyise the molecule. Each molecule of $CH_4$ contains 4 Hydrogen atoms.

Step 2: Scale to a mole. If 1 molecule has 4 atoms, then 1 mole of molecules has 4 moles of atoms.

Step 3: Calculate the total. $$4 \times (6.022 \times 10^{23}) = 2.409 \times 10^{24} \text{ atoms of H}$$

Example 2: Reverse Counting

If you have $3.011 \times 10^{23}$ atoms of Gold, how many moles do you have?

Step 1: Identify the relationship. 1 mole = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms.

Step 2: Set up the division. $$\text{Moles} = \frac{\text{Actual Particles}}{N_A}$$ $$\text{Moles} = \frac{3.011 \times 10^{23}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} = 0.5 \text{ moles}$$

Practice Your "Mole-Sense"

Question 1

[1 Mark]

Which of the following contains the largest number of atoms?

  1. 1 mole of $He$ (Helium)
  2. 1 mole of $O_2$ (Oxygen gas)
  3. 1 mole of $H_2O$ (Water)
  4. 1 mole of $NH_3$ (Ammonia)

Correct Answer: D

Every "1 mole" of a substance has $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. However, Ammonia ($NH_3$) has 4 atoms per particle (1N + 3H). Helium only has 1, $O_2$ has 2, and $H_2O$ has 3.

Question 2

[2 Marks]

Calculate the total number of ions in 2.0 moles of Sodium Chloride ($NaCl$).

Answer: $2.409 \times 10^{24}$ ions

Reasoning: 1 mole of $NaCl$ contains 2 moles of ions ($1 Na^+ + 1 Cl^-$). Therefore, 2.0 moles of $NaCl$ contains 4.0 moles of ions total. $$4.0 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 2.4088 \times 10^{24}$$

Question 3

[3 Marks]

A chemist has a sample containing $1.806 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Oxygen. How many moles of Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$) would this amount of oxygen be enough to create?

First, find out how many moles of Oxygen atoms you have. Then, remember that each $CO_2$ molecule requires 2 Oxygen atoms.

Answer: 1.5 moles of $CO_2$

  1. Moles of Oxygen atoms = $(1.806 \times 10^{24}) / (6.022 \times 10^{23}) = 3.0$ moles of O atoms.
  2. $CO_2$ formula shows 2 O atoms per molecule.
  3. Moles of $CO_2 = 3.0 / 2 = 1.5$ moles.
