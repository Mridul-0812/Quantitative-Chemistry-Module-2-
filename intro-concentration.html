16. Intro to Concentration

Expressing the crowdedness of particles in a solution

The Vocabulary of Solutions

Up until now, we have mostly dealt with solid masses or pure gases. However, the vast majority of chemical reactions occurring in nature, in your body, and in industrial laboratories take place in solution.

Before diving into the mathematics, we must establish the formal vocabulary:

  • Solute: The substance being dissolved (e.g., solid salt crystals).
  • Solvent: The liquid doing the dissolving (e.g., water). If water is the solvent, it is called an aqueous solution ($aq$).
  • Solution: The homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent.
  • Concentration: A mathematical measure of how much solute is dissolved in a specific volume (or mass) of solvent.

💡 Molarity vs. Everything Else

Chemists almost exclusively use Molarity (mol/L) because chemical equations rely on mole ratios. However, in industry, agriculture, and medicine, you will frequently encounter alternative units like grams per litre, percentages, and parts-per-million. You must be fluent in converting between them.

The Six Units of Concentration

Depending on the context, concentration can be expressed in several ways. Memorise the standard formulas below.

UNIT 1: Molarity (M or mol/L)

The holy grail for chemists. It describes the number of moles of solute per litre of solution. We will dedicate the entire next lesson to this.

$$ c = \frac{n}{V} $$

UNIT 2: Grams per Litre (g/L)

Useful for calculating raw material costs. Describes the mass of solid solute dissolved in one litre of total solution.

$$ \text{Conc.} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{Volume (L)}} $$

UNIT 3: Percentage by Mass (w/w)%

Commonly used for solid mixtures (like alloys) or highly concentrated acids. It stands for "weight per weight".

$$ \%(w/w) = \frac{\text{mass solute (g)}}{\text{mass solution (g)}} \times 100 $$

UNIT 4: Percentage by Volume (v/v)%

Used when mixing two liquids together, such as alcohol in water or fragrances in perfumes.

$$ \%(v/v) = \frac{\text{vol solute (mL)}}{\text{vol solution (mL)}} \times 100 $$

UNIT 5: Percentage Mass/Volume (w/v)%

Used extensively in medicine (e.g., IV saline drips). It specifically relates grams to millilitres.

$$ \%(w/v) = \frac{\text{mass solute (g)}}{\text{vol solution (mL)}} \times 100 $$

UNIT 6: Parts Per Million (ppm)

Used for extremely dilute solutions, such as heavy metal contaminants in drinking water. 1 ppm means 1 part solute per 1,000,000 parts solution.

For aqueous solutions (where 1 kg of water $\approx$ 1 L), it simplifies beautifully:

$$ \text{ppm} = \frac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} $$

Note on Parts per Billion (ppb): If ppm is milligrams per litre ($\text{mg/L}$), then ppb is even smaller: micrograms per litre ($\text{\mu g/L}$).

Guided Worked Solutions

Review these examples to see the formal mathematical working required for the HSC. Click "View Solution" to reveal the step-by-step logic.

Example 1: Basic Grams per Litre (Easy)

A student dissolves $14.5 \text{ g}$ of Potassium Nitrate ($KNO_3$) in enough water to make exactly $250 \text{ mL}$ of solution. Calculate the concentration of the solution in $g/L$.

Step 1: Check your units.
Mass is given in grams ($14.5 \text{ g}$). This is correct.
Volume is given in millilitres ($250 \text{ mL}$). We must convert this to Litres by dividing by 1000.
$V = \frac{250}{1000} = 0.250 \text{ L}$

Step 2: Apply the formula.
$$ c = \frac{mass}{Volume} $$ $$ c = \frac{14.5 \text{ g}}{0.250 \text{ L}} $$ $$ c = 58.0 \text{ g/L} $$

Final Answer: The concentration is $\mathbf{58.0 \text{ g/L}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Example 2: Percentage % (w/v) to Molarity (Medium)

A medical saline drip is labelled as $0.90\% \text{ (w/v)}$ Sodium Chloride ($NaCl$). Calculate the Molarity ($mol/L$) of this solution.

This requires unpacking the definition of % (w/v) before applying the molarity formula.

Step 1: Define the percentage.
$0.90\% \text{ (w/v)}$ explicitly means there is $0.90 \text{ g}$ of $NaCl$ dissolved in every $100 \text{ mL}$ of total solution.

Step 2: Convert to g/L (Scale it up to 1 Litre).
If there is $0.90 \text{ g}$ in $100 \text{ mL}$, then in $1000 \text{ mL}$ ($1 \text{ L}$), there will be 10 times as much:
$0.90 \times 10 = 9.0 \text{ g/L}$.

Step 3: Convert mass to moles to find Molarity.
We know we have $9.0 \text{ g}$ of $NaCl$ in exactly $1 \text{ L}$. Let's find how many moles that mass represents.
$MM(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 \text{ g/mol}$
$$ n = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{9.0}{58.44} = 0.1540... \text{ mol} $$

Final Answer: Since we have $0.154 \text{ mol}$ floating in exactly $1 \text{ L}$, the Molarity is $\mathbf{0.15 \text{ M}}$ (or $mol/L$, rounded to 2 sig figs based on the $0.90$ given).

Example 3: Parts Per Million (ppm) in Aqueous Solutions (Hard)

An environmental chemist tests a $500 \text{ mL}$ sample of river water and finds it contains $0.0025 \text{ g}$ of dissolved Lead ($Pb^{2+}$) ions. Calculate the concentration of Lead in parts per million (ppm).

For dilute aqueous solutions, the density of the solution is approximately equal to pure water ($1 \text{ kg/L}$). Therefore, $1 \text{ ppm} \approx 1 \text{ mg/L}$. We need to convert our mass to milligrams, and ensure our volume is in Litres.

Step 1: Convert mass to milligrams.
$m = 0.0025 \text{ g}$
To convert grams to milligrams, multiply by 1000:
$m = 0.0025 \times 1000 = 2.5 \text{ mg}$

Step 2: Convert volume to Litres.
$V = 500 \text{ mL} = 0.500 \text{ L}$

Step 3: Calculate ppm ($mg/L$).
$$ \text{ppm} = \frac{mass \text{ (mg)}}{Volume \text{ (L)}} $$ $$ \text{ppm} = \frac{2.5}{0.500} $$ $$ \text{ppm} = 5.0 $$

Final Answer: The concentration of Lead in the river water is $\mathbf{5.0 \text{ ppm}}$.

Example 4: The Commercial Acid Density Trap (Extension)

Commercial concentrated Sulfuric Acid ($H_2SO_4$) is sold as a $98.0\% \text{ (w/w)}$ solution. The manufacturer states on the bottle that the density of the solution is $1.84 \text{ g/mL}$.

Calculate the Molarity ($mol/L$) of this highly concentrated acid. This is a classic HSC "Band 6" calculation.

To find Molarity ($mol/L$), we need to find the number of moles in exactly $1 \text{ Litre}$ of solution. Let's base our entire calculation on a hypothetical $1.00 \text{ L}$ ($1000 \text{ mL}$) sample.

Step 1: Find the total mass of $1 \text{ Litre}$ of the solution.
We use the density formula: $\rho = \frac{m}{v} \rightarrow m = \rho \times v$
Mass of $1000 \text{ mL} = 1.84 \text{ g/mL} \times 1000 \text{ mL} = 1840 \text{ g}$.
(This means $1 \text{ Litre}$ of this dense acid weighs $1.84 \text{ kg}$!)

Step 2: Find the mass of pure $H_2SO_4$ solute in that Litre.
The solution is not pure; it is $98.0\% \text{ (w/w)}$ pure $H_2SO_4$.
Mass of pure $H_2SO_4 = 98.0\% \text{ of } 1840 \text{ g}$
$m(H_2SO_4) = 0.980 \times 1840 = 1803.2 \text{ g}$.
(So we have $1803.2 \text{ g}$ of actual acid per Litre. This is our $g/L$ concentration.)

Step 3: Convert the mass of solute to Moles.
$MM(H_2SO_4) = (2 \times 1.008) + 32.07 + (4 \times 16.00) = 98.086 \text{ g/mol}$
$$ n = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{1803.2}{98.086} = 18.3838... \text{ mol} $$

Final Answer: Since there are $18.38 \text{ moles}$ in our $1 \text{ Litre}$ sample, the Molarity is $\mathbf{18.4 \text{ M}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Question 1: The Perfumer's Percentage (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A perfumer mixes $15 \text{ mL}$ of pure essential rose oil with $85 \text{ mL}$ of an ethanol solvent to create a perfume base. Calculate the concentration of the essential oil as a percentage by volume $(v/v)\%$.

Hint: The denominator in the $(v/v)\%$ formula is the volume of the total solution, not just the solvent. You must add the solute volume and solvent volume together first!

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Find total volume of solution.
$V_{\text{solution}} = 15 \text{ mL (oil)} + 85 \text{ mL (ethanol)} = 100 \text{ mL total volume}$

Step 2: Calculate $(v/v)\%$.
$$ \% (v/v) = \frac{V_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}} \times 100 $$ $$ \% (v/v) = \frac{15}{100} \times 100 = \mathbf{15\%} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies that the total volume of the solution is $100 \text{ mL}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates the final percentage as $15\%$.

Question 2: Industrial Sugar Vat (Medium)

[3 Marks]

An industrial vat contains $2500 \text{ L}$ of aqueous solution with a sucrose ($C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}$) concentration of $45.0 \text{ g/L}$. Calculate the exact number of moles of sucrose currently dissolved in the vat.

Hint: This is a two-step process. First, find the total mass of sucrose in the vat by multiplying the concentration ($g/L$) by the total volume ($L$). Then, convert that massive number of grams into moles using the Molar Mass of sucrose.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Find total mass of sucrose.
$c = 45.0 \text{ g/L}$, $V = 2500 \text{ L}$
$m = c \times V = 45.0 \times 2500 = 112,500 \text{ g}$

Step 2: Calculate Molar Mass of Sucrose.
$MM(C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}) = (12 \times 12.01) + (22 \times 1.008) + (11 \times 16.00)$
$MM = 144.12 + 22.176 + 176.00 = 342.296 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 3: Convert mass to moles.
$$ n = \frac{m}{MM} $$ $$ n = \frac{112500}{342.296} = 328.66... \text{ mol} $$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{329 \text{ mol}}$ (rounded to 3 sig figs based on the $45.0$ given).

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates the total mass of sucrose in the vat ($112,500 \text{ g}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates the Molar Mass of $C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}$ ($\approx 342.3 \text{ g/mol}$).
  • 3 Marks: Correct final mole calculation ($329 \text{ mol}$).

Question 3: Heavy Metal Contamination (Hard)

[4 Marks]

Mercury is highly toxic. The legal limit for Mercury ($Hg$) in Australian drinking water is $0.001 \text{ ppm}$. A laboratory tests a $4.50 \text{ L}$ sample of water from a bore and finds it contains $12.5 \text{ \mu g}$ (micrograms) of dissolved Mercury.

Calculate the concentration in ppm and state whether the water is legally safe to drink.
(Conversion helper: $1 \text{ mg} = 1000 \text{ \mu g}$).

Hint: Remember that $1 \text{ ppm} = 1 \text{ mg/L}$ for aqueous solutions. You need to convert $12.5 \text{ \mu g}$ into milligrams ($mg$) first by dividing by 1000. Then divide that mass by the total volume in Litres.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert mass to milligrams.
$m = 12.5 \text{ \mu g}$
$m = \frac{12.5}{1000} = 0.0125 \text{ mg}$

Step 2: Calculate ppm ($mg/L$).
$$ \text{ppm} = \frac{\text{mass (mg)}}{\text{Volume (L)}} $$ $$ \text{ppm} = \frac{0.0125 \text{ mg}}{4.50 \text{ L}} = 0.00277... \text{ ppm} $$

Step 3: Evaluate Safety.
Calculated concentration ($0.00278 \text{ ppm}$) is greater than the legal limit ($0.001 \text{ ppm}$).
Final Answer: Concentration is $\mathbf{0.00278 \text{ ppm}}$. The water is NOT safe to drink.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts micrograms to milligrams ($0.0125 \text{ mg}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the ppm formula division ($mg/L$).
  • 3 Marks: Correct final calculation ($0.00278 \text{ ppm}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly states the water is unsafe based on comparing their calculated value to the limit.

Question 4: The Commercial Acid Trick (Extension)

[5 Marks]

Commercial concentrated Nitric Acid ($HNO_3$) is sold as a $70.0\% \text{ (w/w)}$ solution. It is stated on the supplier's Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) that the density of this solution is $1.41 \text{ g/mL}$.

Calculate the Molarity ($mol/L$) of this commercial acid. Provide your answer to 3 significant figures.

Hint: Just like the worked example, assume you have exactly $1 \text{ L}$ ($1000 \text{ mL}$) of the solution. Use the density to find out how much that $1 \text{ L}$ weighs in total grams. Then use the $70.0\%$ to find the mass of pure $HNO_3$ in that Litre. Finally, convert that mass to moles using $n = m/MM$.

Worked Solution:

To find Molarity ($mol/L$), we assume a volume of exactly $1 \text{ Litre}$ ($1000 \text{ mL}$) to make the maths straightforward.

Step 1: Find total mass of $1 \text{ Litre}$ of solution.
Density = $1.41 \text{ g/mL}$.
Mass of $1000 \text{ mL} = 1000 \text{ mL} \times 1.41 \text{ g/mL} = 1410 \text{ g}$.

Step 2: Find the mass of pure $HNO_3$ solute.
The solution is $70.0\% \text{ (w/w)}$ pure $HNO_3$.
Mass of $HNO_3 = 70.0\% \text{ of } 1410 \text{ g}$
Mass of $HNO_3 = 0.700 \times 1410 = 987 \text{ g}$.
(This means the concentration is $987 \text{ g/L}$)

Step 3: Convert mass of $HNO_3$ to Moles.
$MM(HNO_3) = 1.008 + 14.01 + (3 \times 16.00) = 63.018 \text{ g/mol}$
$$ n = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{987}{63.018} = 15.662... \text{ mol} $$

Step 4: State Molarity.
Since there are $15.66 \text{ mol}$ in our assumed $1 \text{ L}$, the Molarity is $\mathbf{15.7 \text{ M}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Logically assumes a volume to work with (e.g., $1 \text{ L}$ or $100 \text{ mL}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly uses density to calculate the total mass of the solution ($1410 \text{ g}$ for $1\text{L}$).
  • 3 Marks: Applies the w/w percentage to find the mass of pure $HNO_3$ ($987 \text{ g}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly determines the Molar Mass of $HNO_3$ and divides the mass by it.
  • 5 Marks: Arrives at the correct final Molarity ($15.7 \text{ mol/L}$).
