11. Mass-Mass Conversions

The Classic "Gram-to-Gram" Three-Step Method

You Can't Compare Apples to Oranges (or Grams to Grams)

In the previous lesson, we built the "Stoichiometric Bridge" using mole ratios. But there is a massive problem: in a real laboratory, you cannot measure out "moles" on an electronic scale. You can only measure mass (grams).

If $1 \text{ mole}$ of Carbon reacts with $1 \text{ mole}$ of Oxygen to make $CO_2$, it does not mean $1 \text{ gram}$ of Carbon reacts with $1 \text{ gram}$ of Oxygen! Why? Because atoms have different masses. One mole of Carbon is $\approx 12 \text{ g}$, but one mole of $O_2$ is $\approx 32 \text{ g}$.

To solve this, we must always convert our starting mass into moles, cross the mole ratio bridge, and then convert the new moles back into mass. We call this the Golden Three-Step Rule.

The 3-Step Stoichiometry Method

1

Mass $\rightarrow$ Moles

Convert the mass of the Known substance (A) into moles.

$n(A) = \frac{m(A)}{MM(A)}$

→
2

The Bridge

Use the balanced equation to find the moles of the Unknown (B).

$n(B) = n(A) \times \frac{\text{Want}}{\text{Have}}$

→
3

Moles $\rightarrow$ Mass

Convert the moles of the Unknown (B) back into mass.

$m(B) = n(B) \times MM(B)$

Crucial Exam Warning!

In your HSC or final exams, examiners will rarely hold your hand. Often, a question will say: "Calculate the mass of precipitate formed when 5.0g of silver nitrate is added to sodium chloride solution."

They will NOT give you the equation. You are expected to:

  1. Predict the products yourself (e.g., precipitation, combustion, acid-base rules).
  2. Write the correct standard states $(s, l, g, aq)$.
  3. Balance the equation perfectly. (If your equation is unbalanced, Step 2 of your stoichiometry will be completely wrong, losing you most of the marks!)

Note: For the sake of practising the maths in this specific lesson, we will provide the reactants, products, and states. However, the equations will be unbalanced. You must balance them before doing the math!

Worked Examples

Example 1: The Standard 3-Step (Easy)

Solid sodium reacts with chlorine gas to produce solid sodium chloride.
Equation: $\_Na(s) + \_Cl_2(g) \rightarrow \_NaCl(s)$
If you start with $11.5 \text{ g}$ of sodium, calculate the mass of sodium chloride produced.

Step 0: Balance the Equation

$$ 2Na(s) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2NaCl(s) $$

Step 1: Mass $\rightarrow$ Moles (of Known)

Known = $Na$. Mass = $11.5 \text{ g}$. $MM(Na) = 22.99 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ n(Na) = \frac{11.5}{22.99} = 0.5002 \text{ mol} $$

Step 2: The Stoichiometric Bridge

Want = $NaCl$ (coeff 2). Have = $Na$ (coeff 2). Ratio = $\frac{2}{2} = 1$.

$$ n(NaCl) = 0.5002 \times 1 = 0.5002 \text{ mol} $$

Step 3: Moles $\rightarrow$ Mass (of Unknown)

$MM(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ m(NaCl) = 0.5002 \times 58.44 = \mathbf{29.2 \text{ g}} $$

Example 2: Fractional Ratios (Medium)

Propane burns in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water vapour.
Equation: $\_C_3H_8(g) + \_O_2(g) \rightarrow \_CO_2(g) + \_H_2O(g)$
What mass of oxygen is required to completely combust $44.1 \text{ g}$ of propane?

Step 0: Balance the Equation

$$ C_3H_8(g) + 5O_2(g) \rightarrow 3CO_2(g) + 4H_2O(g) $$

Step 1: Mass $\rightarrow$ Moles (of Known)

Known = $C_3H_8$. Mass = $44.1 \text{ g}$. $MM = (3 \times 12.01) + (8 \times 1.008) = 44.094 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ n(C_3H_8) = \frac{44.1}{44.094} = 1.000 \text{ mol} $$

Step 2: The Stoichiometric Bridge

Want = $O_2$ (coeff 5). Have = $C_3H_8$ (coeff 1). Ratio = $\frac{5}{1} = 5$.

$$ n(O_2) = 1.000 \times 5 = 5.000 \text{ mol} $$

Step 3: Moles $\rightarrow$ Mass (of Unknown)

$MM(O_2) = 2 \times 16.00 = 32.00 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ m(O_2) = 5.000 \times 32.00 = \mathbf{160.0 \text{ g}} $$

Example 3: Thermal Decomposition (Hard)

Calcium carbonate decomposes when heated strongly to produce solid calcium oxide and carbon dioxide gas.
Equation: $\_CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow \_CaO(s) + \_CO_2(g)$
If $50.0 \text{ g}$ of carbon dioxide gas is collected, what mass of calcium carbonate was decomposed?

Step 0: Balance the Equation

$$ CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g) $$ (Already balanced! $1:1:1$)

Step 1: Mass $\rightarrow$ Moles (of Known)

Known = $CO_2$. Mass = $50.0 \text{ g}$. $MM = 12.01 + (2 \times 16.00) = 44.01 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ n(CO_2) = \frac{50.0}{44.01} = 1.136 \text{ mol} $$

Step 2: The Stoichiometric Bridge

Want = $CaCO_3$. Ratio = $\frac{1}{1} = 1$.

$$ n(CaCO_3) = 1.136 \text{ mol} $$

Step 3: Moles $\rightarrow$ Mass (of Unknown)

$MM(CaCO_3) = 40.08 + 12.01 + (3 \times 16.00) = 100.09 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ m(CaCO_3) = 1.136 \times 100.09 = \mathbf{113.7 \text{ g}} $$

Example 4: Hydrated Salts (Extension)

Copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$) is heated to remove all water, leaving anhydrous $CuSO_4(s)$ and driving off $H_2O(g)$.
If you start with $24.97 \text{ g}$ of the blue hydrated crystals, calculate the mass of the white anhydrous powder remaining.

Step 0: Write and Balance the Equation

$$ CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O(s) \rightarrow CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g) $$

Step 1: Mass $\rightarrow$ Moles (of Hydrate)

$MM(CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O) = 63.55 + 32.07 + (4 \times 16.00) + 5 \times (18.016) = 249.7 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ n(\text{hydrate}) = \frac{24.97}{249.7} = 0.1000 \text{ mol} $$

Step 2: The Stoichiometric Bridge

Want = $CuSO_4$ (anhydrous). Have = Hydrate. Ratio = $\frac{1}{1} = 1$.

$$ n(CuSO_4) = 0.1000 \text{ mol} $$

Step 3: Moles $\rightarrow$ Mass (of Anhydrous)

$MM(CuSO_4) = 63.55 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 159.62 \text{ g/mol}$.

$$ m(CuSO_4) = 0.1000 \times 159.62 = \mathbf{15.96 \text{ g}} $$

Practice Questions

Remember: Balance the equation first!

Question 1: Easy

[3 Marks]

Magnesium burns in oxygen: $\_Mg(s) + \_O_2(g) \rightarrow \_MgO(s)$

Calculate the mass of Magnesium Oxide ($MgO$) produced when $6.00 \text{ g}$ of Magnesium ($Mg$) is completely burnt.

Hint: The balanced equation is $2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO$. The ratio of $Mg$ to $MgO$ is $2:2$, which simplifies to $1:1$.

Worked Solution:

1. Eq: $2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO$

2. $n(Mg) = \frac{6.00}{24.31} = 0.2468 \text{ mol}$

3. Ratio $1:1$, so $n(MgO) = 0.2468 \text{ mol}$

4. $m(MgO) = n \times MM = 0.2468 \times (24.31 + 16.00) = 0.2468 \times 40.31 = \mathbf{9.95 \text{ g}}$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly balances equation and calculates moles of Mg.
  • 1 Mark: Correct application of stoichiometric ratio.
  • 1 Mark: Correct final mass ($9.95 \text{ g}$).

Question 2: Medium

[4 Marks]

Nitrogen gas reacts with hydrogen gas to form ammonia: $\_N_2(g) + \_H_2(g) \rightarrow \_NH_3(g)$

Calculate the mass of hydrogen gas ($H_2$) required to fully react with $14.0 \text{ g}$ of Nitrogen gas ($N_2$).

Hint: Balance the equation ($1:3:2$). Calculate moles of $N_2$ using its diatomic mass ($28.02$). The ratio of Want/Have is $3/1$.

Worked Solution:

1. Eq: $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$

2. $n(N_2) = \frac{14.0}{28.02} = 0.4996 \text{ mol}$

3. $n(H_2) = 0.4996 \times \frac{3}{1} = 1.4989 \text{ mol}$

4. $m(H_2) = 1.4989 \times 2.016 = \mathbf{3.02 \text{ g}}$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly balances the equation.
  • 1 Mark: Correct moles of $N_2$ ($0.4996 \text{ mol}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct moles of $H_2$ using $3:1$ ratio.
  • 1 Mark: Correct final mass ($3.02 \text{ g}$).

Question 3: Hard

[4 Marks]

Aluminium dissolves in hydrochloric acid: $\_Al(s) + \_HCl(aq) \rightarrow \_AlCl_3(aq) + \_H_2(g)$

If an experiment requires $5.00 \text{ g}$ of hydrogen gas ($H_2$) to fill a balloon, what mass of solid Aluminium must be reacted?

Hint: Balancing this is tricky. Start with the $Cl$ and $H$. The balanced equation is $2Al + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2$.

Worked Solution:

1. Eq: $2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) \rightarrow 2AlCl_3(aq) + 3H_2(g)$

2. $n(H_2) = \frac{5.00}{2.016} = 2.480 \text{ mol}$

3. Ratio (Want $Al$ / Have $H_2$) = $\frac{2}{3}$.
$n(Al) = 2.480 \times \frac{2}{3} = 1.653 \text{ mol}$

4. $m(Al) = 1.653 \times 26.98 = \mathbf{44.6 \text{ g}}$

  • 1 Mark: Correct balanced equation ($2,6,2,3$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct moles of $H_2$ ($2.48 \text{ mol}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct stoichiometric ratio ($\times 2/3$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct final mass of Aluminium ($44.6 \text{ g}$).

Question 4: Extension

[5 Marks]

Octane ($C_8H_{18}$) undergoes complete combustion. $\_C_8H_{18}(l) + \_O_2(g) \rightarrow \_CO_2(g) + \_H_2O(g)$

If $1.00 \text{ kg}$ ($1000 \text{ g}$) of octane is burnt in a car engine, calculate the total combined mass of the greenhouse gases ($CO_2$ and $H_2O$) emitted from the exhaust.

Hint: Balance the equation ($2:25:16:18$). Calculate moles of octane. Then you have to do the bridge and mass calculation TWICE (once for $CO_2$, once for $H_2O$), then add the final masses together.

Worked Solution:

1. Eq: $2C_8H_{18} + 25O_2 \rightarrow 16CO_2 + 18H_2O$

2. $MM(C_8H_{18}) = (8 \times 12.01) + (18 \times 1.008) = 114.224 \text{ g/mol}$

$n(C_8H_{18}) = \frac{1000}{114.224} = 8.755 \text{ mol}$

3. Calculate $CO_2$:

$n(CO_2) = 8.755 \times \frac{16}{2} = 70.04 \text{ mol}$

$m(CO_2) = 70.04 \times 44.01 = 3082.5 \text{ g}$

4. Calculate $H_2O$:

$n(H_2O) = 8.755 \times \frac{18}{2} = 78.795 \text{ mol}$

$m(H_2O) = 78.795 \times 18.016 = 1419.6 \text{ g}$

5. Total Mass:

Total $= 3082.5 + 1419.6 = \mathbf{4502.1 \text{ g}}$ (or $4.50 \text{ kg}$)

  • 1 Mark: Correct balanced equation ($2,25,16,18$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct moles of Octane ($8.755 \text{ mol}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct mass of $CO_2$ ($\approx 3.08 \text{ kg}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct mass of $H_2O$ ($\approx 1.42 \text{ kg}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct total mass summed up.
