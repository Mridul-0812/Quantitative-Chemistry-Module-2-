28. Gas Stoichiometry

Combining Moles, Mass, and Gas Volumes in Chemical Reactions

The Climax of Module 2

Welcome to the summit of Quantitative Chemistry. Up until now, you have learned how to calculate reacting masses (Phase C), reacting concentrations (Phase D), and properties of isolated gases (Phase E). Now, we merge them all.

Everything Comes Back to "Haunt" You!

Limiting Reagents. Molar Masses. Balancing Equations. Empirical Formulas. They are all returning in this final sub-topic. But view this as a solace, not a fright! You are not learning any new mathematics today. You are simply applying the tools you have already mastered—like $PV=nRT$ and $n=m/MM$—to solve comprehensive chemical puzzles.

When you encounter a gas stoichiometry problem, simply ask yourself: "Do I need to find Moles, or can I use a shortcut?"

Shortcut: Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes

If a chemical reaction involves ONLY gases, and the temperature and pressure are held constant throughout the reaction, Avogadro's Law ($V \propto n$) gifts us an incredible shortcut.

Because volume is directly proportional to moles, the stoichiometric coefficients in a balanced equation dictate the exact Volume Ratio of the reacting gases! You do not need to calculate moles at all.

Example: The Synthesis of Ammonia

$N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)$

Mole Ratio: $1 \text{ mol} : 3 \text{ mol} \rightarrow 2 \text{ mol}$

Volume Ratio: $1 \text{ L} : 3 \text{ L} \rightarrow 2 \text{ L}$

If you react $10 \text{ L}$ of Nitrogen, you will instantly know it requires $30 \text{ L}$ of Hydrogen and produces exactly $20 \text{ L}$ of Ammonia. No $PV=nRT$ required!

Mixed Stoichiometry: When Solids meet Gases

You cannot use volume ratios if a reactant or product is a solid or a liquid! (e.g., $CaCO_3(s)$ reacting to form $CO_2(g)$). The volume of a solid is irrelevant to gas laws.

If you are given the mass of a solid and asked for the volume of a gas, you must navigate through the mole. Moles are the universal currency of chemistry.

  • Step 1: Convert the known substance into moles (using $n = m/MM$ or $n = c \times V$).
  • Step 2: Use the stoichiometric ratio from the balanced equation to find the moles of the unknown gas.
  • Step 3: Convert the moles of the gas into Volume using $PV = nRT$ (or $V = n \times 24.79$ if strictly at SLC).

Finding Empirical Formulas with Gases

A classic HSC trick question involves combusting an unknown hydrocarbon gas ($C_xH_y$) in Oxygen, and measuring the volume of Carbon Dioxide and Water Vapour produced. Because everything is a gas, you can use the volume ratios to find the empirical formula backwards!

If $10 \text{ mL}$ of a hydrocarbon produces $30 \text{ mL}$ of $CO_2$, the volume ratio is $1:3$. This means every 1 molecule of hydrocarbon contains 3 atoms of Carbon ($x=3$). We will explore this in the Extension examples below.

Worked Solutions

Review the meticulous application of stoichiometry combined with gas laws. Note how we distinguish between problems that allow volume ratios and those that mandate mole calculations.

Easy

Example 1: Pure Volume Ratios

Hydrogen gas reacts with Chlorine gas to form Hydrogen Chloride gas according to the equation:
$H_2(g) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2HCl(g)$

If $12.5 \text{ L}$ of Hydrogen gas completely reacts at constant temperature and pressure, calculate the volume of $HCl$ gas produced.

Worked Solution:

Because all reactants and products are gases at constant $T$ and $P$, we can use Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes. We do not need to calculate moles.

Step 1: Identify the Volume Ratio
From the balanced equation, the ratio of $H_2 : HCl$ is $1 : 2$.

Step 2: Calculate Product Volume
Volume of $HCl$ produced = Volume of $H_2 \times 2$
$V(HCl) = 12.5 \text{ L} \times 2 = 25.0 \text{ L}$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{25.0 \text{ L}}$ of $HCl$ gas is produced.

Medium

Example 2: Solid to Gas (Mixed Stoichiometry)

Sodium metal reacts violently with water to produce aqueous Sodium Hydroxide and Hydrogen gas:
$2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g)$

Calculate the volume of Hydrogen gas produced at STP ($0 ^\circ\text{C}, 100 \text{kPa}$) when $4.60 \text{ g}$ of Sodium completely reacts.

Worked Solution:

Since we are starting with a solid mass, we MUST calculate moles first.

Step 1: Calculate moles of Reactant ($Na$)
$MM(Na) = 22.99 \text{ g/mol}$
$n(Na) = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{4.60}{22.99} = 0.20008... \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Mole Ratio
Ratio of $Na : H_2$ is $2 : 1$.
$n(H_2) = \frac{0.20008}{2} = 0.10004... \text{ mol}$

Step 3: Convert Gas Moles to Volume at STP
Because the conditions are strictly STP, we can use the Molar Volume shortcut ($V_m = 22.71 \text{ L/mol}$).
$V(H_2) = n \times V_m$
$V(H_2) = 0.10004 \times 22.71 = 2.271... \text{ L}$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{2.27 \text{ L}}$ of Hydrogen gas is produced (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Limiting Reagents with Gases

Ammonia gas reacts with Oxygen gas to form Nitrogen Monoxide and Water Vapour:
$4NH_3(g) + 5O_2(g) \rightarrow 4NO(g) + 6H_2O(g)$

In a sealed reaction vessel at constant temperature and pressure, $20.0 \text{ L}$ of Ammonia is mixed with $20.0 \text{ L}$ of Oxygen and ignited. Determine the limiting reagent and calculate the final volume of $NO$ gas produced.

Worked Solution:

Because all substances are gases at constant conditions, we can do the Limiting Reagent calculation entirely using volumes instead of moles!

Step 1: Find the Limiting Reagent
Divide the available volume of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient:
For $NH_3$: $\frac{20.0 \text{ L}}{4} = 5.00$
For $O_2$: $\frac{20.0 \text{ L}}{5} = 4.00$
Since $4.00 < 5.00$, Oxygen ($O_2$) is the Limiting Reagent.

Step 2: Calculate Product Volume based on LR
We use the volume of $O_2$ ($20.0 \text{ L}$) to find the volume of $NO$.
Ratio of $O_2 : NO$ is $5 : 4$.
$V(NO) = V(O_2) \times \frac{4}{5}$
$V(NO) = 20.0 \times \frac{4}{5} = 16.0 \text{ L}$

Final Answer: The limiting reagent is $O_2$, and $\mathbf{16.0 \text{ L}}$ of $NO$ gas is produced.

Extension

Example 4: Unknown Empirical Formula

Exactly $15.0 \text{ mL}$ of an unknown gaseous hydrocarbon ($C_xH_y$) undergoes complete combustion in excess Oxygen. The products are cooled to $120 ^\circ\text{C}$ (ensuring water remains a gas). The combustion yields $45.0 \text{ mL}$ of Carbon Dioxide gas and $60.0 \text{ mL}$ of Water Vapour. All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.

Determine the empirical formula of the hydrocarbon.

Worked Solution:

Since conditions are constant, the volume ratios directly represent the mole ratios (coefficients) in the balanced chemical equation.

Step 1: Write the general skeleton equation
$1 C_xH_y(g) + zO_2(g) \rightarrow xCO_2(g) + \frac{y}{2}H_2O(g)$

Step 2: Determine the Volume Ratios
Hydrocarbon : $CO_2$ : $H_2O$
$15.0 \text{ mL} : 45.0 \text{ mL} : 60.0 \text{ mL}$

Step 3: Simplify the ratio to find coefficients
Divide all volumes by the volume of the hydrocarbon ($15.0$):
$1 : 3 : 4$
This means 1 mole of hydrocarbon produces 3 moles of $CO_2$ and 4 moles of $H_2O$.

Step 4: Solve for $x$ and $y$
Carbon: All carbon goes into $CO_2$. Since there are 3 moles of $CO_2$, there must be 3 Carbon atoms. $\therefore x = 3$.
Hydrogen: All hydrogen goes into $H_2O$. Since there are 4 moles of $H_2O$ (which each contain 2 hydrogens), there must be $4 \times 2 = 8$ Hydrogen atoms. $\therefore y = 8$.

Final Answer: The empirical formula is $\mathbf{C_3H_8}$ (Propane).

Practise Questions

Put everything together. Identify when to use shortcuts and when to calculate moles. Write your working out on paper, explicitly checking your states ($s, l, aq, g$), reveal the solutions, and self-assess using the criteria.

Question 1: Pure Gas Reactants (Easy)

[2 Marks]

Nitrogen gas reacts with Oxygen gas to form Nitrogen Dioxide:
$N_2(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow 2NO_2(g)$

If $8.50 \text{ L}$ of Nitrogen gas completely reacts at constant temperature and pressure, calculate the volume of Nitrogen Dioxide gas produced.

Hint: Everything is a gas! You don't need to find moles. Just look at the ratio of $N_2$ to $NO_2$ in the equation and multiply the volume by that ratio.

Worked Solution:

Ratio of $N_2 : NO_2$ is $1 : 2$.

$$ V(NO_2) = V(N_2) \times 2 $$

$$ V(NO_2) = 8.50 \times 2 $$

$$ V(NO_2) = \mathbf{17.0 \text{ L}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies the 1:2 volume ratio between the reactant and product.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final volume calculated ($17.0 \text{ L}$).

Question 2: Solid to Gas via PV=nRT (Medium)

[4 Marks]

Magnesium metal reacts with excess Hydrochloric Acid:
$Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)$

Calculate the volume of Hydrogen gas produced when $12.5 \text{ g}$ of Magnesium completely reacts in a lab at $28.0 ^\circ\text{C}$ and $102 \text{ kPa}$.

Hint:
1. Calculate Moles of Mg ($m/MM$).
2. Find Moles of $H_2$ using the $1:1$ ratio.
3. Because the conditions ($28^\circ\text{C}$, $102\text{kPa}$) are NOT STP or SLC, you MUST use $V = nRT/P$ to find the final volume!

Worked Solution:

1. Moles of Mg:
$n(Mg) = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{12.5}{24.31} = 0.51419... \text{ mol}$

2. Moles of H₂:
Ratio $Mg : H_2$ is $1 : 1$.
$n(H_2) = 0.51419... \text{ mol}$

3. Calculate Volume via Ideal Gas Law:
$T = 28.0 + 273.15 = 301.15 \text{ K}$
$P = 102 \text{ kPa}$
$$ V = \frac{nRT}{P} $$ $$ V = \frac{0.51419 \times 8.314 \times 301.15}{102} $$ $$ V = \frac{1287.41...}{102} = \mathbf{12.62... \text{ L}} $$ Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{12.6 \text{ L}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates moles of Mg ($\approx 0.514 \text{ mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Determines moles of Hydrogen are equal ($1:1$ ratio).
  • 3 Marks: Converts temperature to Kelvin and sets up $PV=nRT$ equation.
  • 4 Marks: Correct final volume calculated ($\approx 12.6 \text{ L}$).

Question 3: Limiting Reagents with Mixed States (Hard)

[5 Marks]

Calcium Carbonate reacts with Hydrochloric Acid:
$CaCO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + CO_2(g) + H_2O(l)$

If $15.0 \text{ g}$ of $CaCO_3$ is reacted with $100 \text{ mL}$ of $2.00 \text{ M}$ $HCl$ at Standard Laboratory Conditions (SLC), determine the limiting reagent and calculate the volume of $CO_2$ gas produced.

Hint:
1. Find moles of $CaCO_3$ ($m/MM$).
2. Find moles of $HCl$ ($c \times V$, convert V to Litres).
3. Divide by coefficients to find the Limiting Reagent.
4. Use the moles of the LR to find moles of $CO_2$.
5. Use the SLC shortcut ($V = n \times 24.79$) to find the volume.

Worked Solution:

1. Moles of Reactants:
$MM(CaCO_3) = 100.09 \text{ g/mol}$
$n(CaCO_3) = \frac{15.0}{100.09} = 0.14986... \text{ mol}$

$n(HCl) = c \times V = 2.00 \times 0.100 = 0.200 \text{ mol}$

2. Identify Limiting Reagent:
Required ratio $CaCO_3 : HCl$ is $1 : 2$.
Testing $HCl$: $\frac{0.200}{2} = 0.100$
Since $0.100 < 0.1498$, $HCl$ is the Limiting Reagent.

3. Calculate Moles of Product ($CO_2$):
Ratio $HCl : CO_2$ is $2 : 1$.
$n(CO_2) = \frac{n(HCl)}{2} = \frac{0.200}{2} = 0.100 \text{ mol}$

4. Convert Moles to Volume at SLC:
Conditions are SLC, so $V_m = 24.79 \text{ L/mol}$.
$$ V(CO_2) = n \times 24.79 $$ $$ V = 0.100 \times 24.79 = \mathbf{2.479 \text{ L}} $$ Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{2.48 \text{ L}}$.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Calculates correct moles of $CaCO_3$ ($\approx 0.150 \text{ mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Calculates correct moles of $HCl$ ($0.200 \text{ mol}$).
  • 3 Marks: Correctly identifies $HCl$ as the limiting reagent.
  • 4 Marks: Uses the limiting reagent to find moles of $CO_2$ ($0.100 \text{ mol}$).
  • 5 Marks: Correctly applies the SLC volume shortcut to find final volume ($\approx 2.48 \text{ L}$).

Question 4: Combustion Empirical Formula (Extension)

[4 Marks]

Exactly $20.0 \text{ mL}$ of an unknown gaseous hydrocarbon ($C_xH_y$) is combusted in excess Oxygen. The products are cooled to $150 ^\circ\text{C}$ at $101.3 \text{ kPa}$. The combustion yields exactly $80.0 \text{ mL}$ of Carbon Dioxide gas and $100.0 \text{ mL}$ of Water Vapour.

Determine the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon, and state its IUPAC name.

Hint: 1. Divide the volume of $CO_2$ by the volume of hydrocarbon to find $x$ (Carbon atoms).
2. Divide the volume of $H_2O$ by the volume of hydrocarbon to find the coefficient of water. Multiply that coefficient by 2 to find $y$ (Hydrogen atoms).
3. Write the formula $C_xH_y$ and name it using your organic chemistry prefixes (Meth, Eth, Prop, But...).

Worked Solution:

1. Write the general skeleton equation:
$1 C_xH_y(g) + zO_2(g) \rightarrow xCO_2(g) + \frac{y}{2}H_2O(g)$

2. Determine the Volume Ratios:
Hydrocarbon : $CO_2$ : $H_2O$
$20.0 \text{ mL} : 80.0 \text{ mL} : 100.0 \text{ mL}$

3. Simplify to find coefficients:
Divide all by $20.0$:
$1 : 4 : 5$
This means 1 mole of hydrocarbon produces 4 moles of $CO_2$ and 5 moles of $H_2O$.

4. Solve for $x$ and $y$:
Carbon: All carbon goes into $CO_2$. $\implies x = 4$.
Hydrogen: All hydrogen goes into $H_2O$. There are 5 moles of $H_2O$, each containing 2 hydrogens. $\implies y = 5 \times 2 = 10$.

Final Answer: The formula is $\mathbf{C_4H_{10}}$. The IUPAC name is Butane.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Simplifies the volumes into a workable 1:4:5 ratio.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly determines the number of Carbon atoms ($x=4$).
  • 3 Marks: Correctly determines the number of Hydrogen atoms ($y=10$), remembering to multiply the water coefficient by 2.
  • 4 Marks: Correctly identifies the formula as $C_4H_{10}$ and names it Butane.
