3. Balancing Rules

The Law of Conservation & Chemical Accounting

The Law of Conservation of Mass

In 1789, Antoine Lavoisier changed chemistry forever by proving a simple, yet profound truth: Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Imagine the Universe as a very strict accountant. Every single atom that enters a reaction (as a reactant) must be present when the reaction is finished (as a product). They might be bonded differently, and they might look like a completely new substance, but the total number of atoms for each element must remain constant.

[Image of the Law of Conservation of Mass experiment]
  • Reactants: The "Before" state. Atoms ready to shuffle.
  • Products: The "After" state. The same atoms, newly arranged.
  • The Arrow (→): Represents a chemical change (not an equals sign, though mathematically we treat it as one for balancing).

The Golden Rules of Balancing

Balancing is like a puzzle where you are only allowed to move specific pieces. If you break these rules, you aren't just getting the math wrong—you're breaking the laws of physics!

Rule #1: Never Touch Subscripts

The subscript (the small number, like the '2' in $H_2O$) defines the substance. If you change $H_2O$ to $H_2O_2$ to balance an oxygen, you have turned life-giving water into hair bleach/rocket fuel. Subscripts are sacred.

Rule #2: Only Change Coefficients

The coefficient is the large number in front of a molecule (e.g., $2H_2O$). This tells us how many whole molecules we have. You can change these as much as you like.

Rule #3: The Final Count

A balanced equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow.

The MINOH Strategy: A helpful order to balance atoms is:
1. Metals
2. Ions (polyatomic ions that stay together)
3. Non-metals
4. Oxygen
5. Hydrogen

Worked Solutions

Mastering balancing requires a systematic approach. Follow these step-by-step examples.

Example 1: Simple Synthesis

Balance the equation for the formation of Ammonia: $$N_2 + H_2 \rightarrow NH_3$$

Step 1: Inventory the atoms.

Reactants: N=2, H=2 | Products: N=1, H=3

Step 2: Balance Nitrogen.

We have 2 Nitrogen on the left, so we need a coefficient of 2 in front of $NH_3$.

$$N_2 + H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$$

Current Inventory: N=2, H=2 | Products: N=2, H=6

Step 3: Balance Hydrogen.

We need 6 Hydrogens on the left. Since each $H_2$ has 2, we need a coefficient of 3 ($3 \times 2 = 6$).

$$N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$$

Final Check: 2N and 6H on both sides. Success!

Example 2: Using Polyatomic Ions

Balance the following double displacement reaction: $$AgNO_3 + MgCl_2 \rightarrow AgCl + Mg(NO_3)_2$$

Tip: If a polyatomic ion (like $NO_3$) appears on both sides, balance it as a single unit!

Step 1: Balance Nitrate ($NO_3$).

There are 2 on the right, so put a 2 on the left: $$2AgNO_3 + MgCl_2 \rightarrow AgCl + Mg(NO_3)_2$$

Step 2: Balance Silver ($Ag$).

Now there are 2 Silver on the left, so put a 2 on the right: $$2AgNO_3 + MgCl_2 \rightarrow 2AgCl + Mg(NO_3)_2$$

Step 3: Check Magnesium and Chlorine.

Left: 1 Mg, 2 Cl | Right: 1 Mg, 2 Cl. Done!

Example 3: Combustion of Methane

Balance the combustion of methane: $$CH_4 + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + H_2O$$

Step 1: Balance Carbon. Carbon is already 1 on both sides.

Step 2: Balance Hydrogen. There are 4 on the left, so we need a 2 on the right: $$CH_4 + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$$

Step 3: Balance Oxygen. On the right, we now have $2$ (from $CO_2$) + $2$ (from $2H_2O$) = 4 Oxygen atoms.

Place a 2 on the left: $$CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$$

Practice Questions

Apply the rules of balancing to the following reactions.

Question 1

[1 Mark]

Identify which of the following is the only allowed action when balancing a chemical equation:

  1. Changing the subscripts of the products.
  2. Adding a new product to account for missing atoms.
  3. Adjusting the coefficients in front of molecules.
  4. Removing atoms that are not needed.

Correct Answer: C

Adjusting coefficients is the only valid way to balance without changing the chemical identity of the substances involved.

Question 2

[1 Mark]

State the coefficient required for $O_2$ to balance the following reaction: $$2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + \underline{\quad} O_2$$

Answer: 1 (though usually left blank, the coefficient is 1).

Left side: 4H, 4O. Right side: 4H, (2+2)O = 4O.

Question 3

[2 Marks]

Balance the following reaction between Aluminium and Hydrochloric Acid: $$Al + HCl \rightarrow AlCl_3 + H_2$$

Hint: Chlorine is 3 on the right and 1 on the left. Hydrogen is 2 on the right and 1 on the left. Look for a common multiple for 2 and 3!

Balanced Equation: $$2Al + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2$$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies that a coefficient of 6 is needed for $HCl$ to resolve the $Cl/H$ ratio.
  • 2 Marks: Entirely balanced equation.

Question 4

[3 Marks]

Explain how the balancing of chemical equations relates to the Law of Conservation of Mass. Use the equation $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$ in your explanation.

The Law of Conservation of Mass states that mass cannot be created or destroyed. In a balanced chemical equation, the number of atoms of each element is identical on both the reactant and product sides. For example, in $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$, there are 4 Hydrogen atoms and 2 Oxygen atoms on both sides. This ensures the total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products, satisfying the law.

  • 1 Mark: Correctly defines the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • 2 Marks: Explains that balancing ensures the number of atoms of each element remains constant.
  • 3 Marks: Accurately uses the provided equation to demonstrate the atom count remains equal.
