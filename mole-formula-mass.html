6. The Mole Formula (Mass)

Bridging the Gap: Mass $\leftrightarrow$ Moles

The Most Important Formula in Chemistry

In the previous lesson, we learned that a mole is a specific quantity of particles ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$). But we cannot physically count atoms in a laboratory. The only tool we have to determine the amount of a solid substance is an electronic mass balance.

Therefore, we need a mathematical bridge to convert the mass we can weigh (in grams) into the number of moles (the quantity of particles). This bridge is provided by the substance's Molar Mass ($MM$).

$n = \frac{m}{MM}$

  • $n$ (moles): The amount of substance. Measured in mol.
  • $m$ (mass): The physical weight of your sample. Must be measured in grams (g).
  • $MM$ (Molar Mass): The mass of exactly one mole of the substance. Sourced from the periodic table. Measured in g/mol.

If you prefer visual aids for rearranging algebra, the "Magic Triangle" is highly effective. Cover the variable you want to find, and the remaining variables show you the mathematical operation required.

m
n MM

Cover 'n': $n = m \div MM$  |  Cover 'm': $m = n \times MM$  |  Cover 'MM': $MM = m \div n$

Where do we find $MM$?

The Molar Mass ($MM$) is not something you memorise. It is provided directly on the NESA Periodic Table as the Atomic Weight.

For elements, the $MM$ is simply the atomic weight. For compounds, it is the sum of the atomic weights of all constituent atoms (as covered in Lesson 2). Below is a highly accurate slice of the p-block from the Periodic Table. Note that values are typically given to 4 significant figures (4 s.f.).

6 C Carbon 12.01
7 N Nitrogen 14.01
8 O Oxygen 16.00
9 F Fluorine 19.00
14 Si Silicon 28.09
15 P Phosphorus 30.97
16 S Sulfur 32.07
17 Cl Chlorine 35.45

Notice that Oxygen ($16.00$) and Carbon ($12.01$) are listed exactly to 4 significant figures, as required by NESA standards. Always use these exact values in your calculations, do not round them prematurely!

Worked Solutions

Familiarise yourself with the standard layouts for mass-mole calculations before proceeding to the practice questions.

Example 1: Finding Moles (Easy)

Calculate the number of moles present in exactly $24.02 \text{ g}$ of pure Carbon.

Step 1: Identify given variables.

$m = 24.02 \text{ g}$

$MM(C) = 12.01 \text{ g/mol}$ (from Periodic Table)

Step 2: Apply the formula.

$$n = \frac{m}{MM}$$

$$n = \frac{24.02}{12.01}$$

$$n = 2.000 \text{ mol}$$

Example 2: Finding Mass (Medium)

Determine the mass of $0.450 \text{ moles}$ of Sodium Chloride ($NaCl$).

Step 1: Calculate $MM$ of the compound.

$MM(NaCl) = 22.99 (Na) + 35.45 (Cl) = 58.44 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 2: Rearrange formula.

From $n = \frac{m}{MM}$, we rearrange to $m = n \times MM$

Step 3: Substitute and solve.

$$m = 0.450 \times 58.44$$

$$m = 26.3 \text{ g}$$ (rounded to 3 significant figures based on the given $0.450$ moles)

Example 3: Diatomic Traps (Hard)

A balloon contains $64.00 \text{ g}$ of oxygen gas. How many moles of gas are in the balloon?

Step 1: Identify the chemical formula.

Oxygen gas is a diatomic molecule, meaning its formula is $O_2$, not just $O$.

Step 2: Calculate Molar Mass.

$MM(O_2) = 2 \times 16.00 = 32.00 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 3: Apply the formula.

$$n = \frac{m}{MM}$$

$$n = \frac{64.00}{32.00} = 2.000 \text{ mol}$$

Common Mistake: Using $MM = 16.00$ would incorrectly give 4 moles. Always remember HONClBrIF for diatomic elements!

Example 4: Unit Conversion (Extension)

An industrial plant produces $1.50 \text{ tonnes}$ of Ammonia ($NH_3$) per day. Calculate the number of moles produced. (1 tonne = 1000 kg).

Step 1: Convert mass strictly into grams.

$m = 1.50 \text{ tonnes} = 1500 \text{ kg} = 1,500,000 \text{ g}$

In scientific notation: $m = 1.50 \times 10^6 \text{ g}$

Step 2: Calculate $MM$ of Ammonia.

$MM(NH_3) = 14.01 + (3 \times 1.008) = 17.034 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 3: Solve.

$$n = \frac{1.50 \times 10^6}{17.034}$$

$$n = 88,059 \text{ mol}$$ (or $8.81 \times 10^4 \text{ mol}$ to 3 s.f.)

Practice Questions

Use your NESA Periodic Table. Pay strict attention to units and significant figures. Assign yourself marks based on the criteria.

Question 1

[1 Mark]

Rearrange the mole formula ($n = \frac{m}{MM}$) to make Molar Mass ($MM$) the subject.

Hint: Use the Magic Triangle. Cover up $MM$. What is left over?

Model Answer:

$$MM = \frac{m}{n}$$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly rearranged algebraic formula.

Question 2

[2 Marks]

Calculate the number of moles in $100.0 \text{ g}$ of Calcium Carbonate ($CaCO_3$).

Hint: First, find the Molar Mass by adding the masses of 1 Ca, 1 C, and 3 O atoms.

Worked Solution:

$MM(CaCO_3) = 40.08 + 12.01 + (3 \times 16.00) = 100.09 \text{ g/mol}$

$$n = \frac{100.0}{100.09} = 0.9991 \text{ mol}$$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates the molar mass of $CaCO_3$ as $100.09 \text{ g/mol}$.
  • 2 Marks: Provides correct final moles, rounded to 4 significant figures ($0.9991$).

Question 3

[3 Marks]

An unknown pure metal sample weighs $104.5 \text{ g}$. A mass spectrometer determines that the sample contains exactly $0.500 \text{ moles}$ of the substance. Identify the metal.

Hint: Use the rearranged formula $MM = m/n$ to find the molar mass. Then, look for that number on your periodic table.

Worked Solution:

$$MM = \frac{m}{n}$$

$$MM = \frac{104.5}{0.500}$$

$$MM = 209.0 \text{ g/mol}$$

Looking at the periodic table, the element with an atomic mass of $209.0$ is Bismuth (Bi).

  • 1 Mark: Substitutes the mass and moles into the correct rearranged formula.
  • 2 Marks: Calculates the Molar Mass as $209.0 \text{ g/mol}$.
  • 3 Marks: Correctly identifies the element as Bismuth.

Question 4

[4 Marks]

Calculate the mass in grams of $2.5 \times 10^{-3} \text{ moles}$ of Magnesium Phosphate, $Mg_3(PO_4)_2$. Give your answer to 2 significant figures.

Hint: 1. Careful calculating the $MM$! You have 3 Mg, 2 P, and 8 O atoms.
2. Use $m = n \times MM$.
3. Ensure your final rounding is strictly to 2 sig figs as requested.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Calculate MM

$MM = (3 \times 24.31) + (2 \times 30.97) + (8 \times 16.00)$

$MM = 72.93 + 61.94 + 128.00 = 262.87 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 2: Calculate Mass

$$m = n \times MM$$

$$m = (2.5 \times 10^{-3}) \times 262.87$$

$$m = 0.657175 \text{ g}$$

Step 3: Rounding

Rounded to 2 significant figures: $m = 0.66 \text{ g}$

  • 1 Mark: Identifies correct number of constituent atoms (3 Mg, 2 P, 8 O).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates $MM = 262.87 \text{ g/mol}$.
  • 3 Marks: Applies formula to find unrounded mass ($0.657... \text{ g}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly rounds final answer to 2 sig figs ($0.66 \text{ g}$).
