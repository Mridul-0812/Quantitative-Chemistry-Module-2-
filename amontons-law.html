23. Amontons' Law

The Mathematical Relationship Between Pressure and Temperature (Gay-Lussac's Law)

The Rigid Chamber

We have explored what happens when Volume is manipulated (Boyle's Law) and when it is allowed to expand freely (Charles' Law). Now, we will investigate a system where the Volume is strictly locked. Imagine a sealed, rigid metal canister—like a scuba tank or an aerosol can.

This relationship is historically attributed to Guillaume Amontons, though it was later quantified precisely by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac. In modern HSC Chemistry, you will hear it referred to by both names.

Amontons' Law (Gay Lussac's Law) Definition

For a fixed mass of gas (constant moles, $n$) held at a strictly constant volume ($V$), the pressure ($P$) of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature ($T$) in Kelvin.

According to the Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT), heating a gas increases the kinetic energy of its particles. Because the walls of the container are rigid and cannot expand to absorb this energy, the faster-moving particles strike the walls much more frequently and with significantly greater force. The result is a sharp increase in internal pressure.

Mathematical Derivation

Since Pressure ($P$) is directly proportional to Temperature ($T$), we write:

$$ P \propto T $$

Introducing a constant of proportionality ($k$):

$$ P = k \times T \quad \implies \quad \frac{P}{T} = k $$

This means that dividing the Pressure of a rigid system by its absolute Temperature always yields the same constant. Thus, comparing two states of the same gas system gives us the working formula for Amontons' Law:

$$ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} $$

CRITICAL: Temperature must ALWAYS be converted to Kelvin ($K$) before using this formula.

The Future Link: The Ideal Gas Law

You may recall from our previous lessons that we are systematically unpacking the variables that make up the master Ideal Gas Law equation: $PV = nRT$.

Let's prove Amontons' Law using this master equation. If we rearrange $PV = nRT$ to group our variables ($P$ and $T$) on one side, and our constants on the other, we get:

$$ \frac{P}{T} = \frac{nR}{V} $$

  • $n$ is the fixed amount of gas (constant).
  • $V$ is the rigid volume of the container (constant).
  • $R$ is the Universal Gas Constant, provided on your NESA data sheet as $8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$.

Because $n$, $R$, and $V$ are all constant in this scenario, the entire right side of the equation ($\frac{nR}{V}$) evaluates to a single constant number ($k$). Therefore, $\frac{P}{T} = k$. This proves that the relationship between pressure and temperature is perfectly linear when volume is locked!

Worked Solutions

Review the mathematical methodology used to solve these problems. Always ensure your temperatures are converted to Kelvin before attempting to cross-multiply fractions.

Easy

Example 1: Direct Substitution

A sealed aerosol can contains gas at a pressure of $105 \text{ kPa}$ at a temperature of $300 \text{ K}$. If the can is left in direct sunlight and heats up to $350 \text{ K}$, calculate the new pressure inside the can.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Extract the data
$P_1 = 105 \text{ kPa}$
$T_1 = 300 \text{ K}$
$P_2 = \text{ ?}$
$T_2 = 350 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Apply Amontons' Law
$$ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{105}{300} = \frac{P_2}{350} $$

Step 3: Rearrange and solve
Multiply both sides by $350$ to isolate $P_2$:
$$ P_2 = \left(\frac{105}{300}\right) \times 350 $$ $$ P_2 = 0.350 \times 350 $$ $$ P_2 = 122.5 \text{ kPa} $$

Final Answer: The new pressure inside the can is $\mathbf{123 \text{ kPa}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Medium

Example 2: The Celsius Trap

A rigid scuba tank is filled with air at $20.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ to a pressure of $200 \text{ atm}$. During a deep dive in cold water, the temperature of the tank drops to $5.00 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the new pressure of the air inside the tank.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert Temperatures to Kelvin!
$T_1 = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 \text{ K}$
$T_2 = 5.00 + 273.15 = 278.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Extract the pressure data
$P_1 = 200 \text{ atm}$
$P_2 = \text{ ?}$

Step 3: Apply the Formula
$$ \frac{200}{293.15} = \frac{P_2}{278.15} $$

Step 4: Solve for $P_2$
$$ P_2 = \left(\frac{200}{293.15}\right) \times 278.15 $$ $$ P_2 = 189.76... \text{ atm} $$

Final Answer: The pressure drops to $\mathbf{190 \text{ atm}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Double Conversion (Finding Celsius)

A sealed glass bulb contains a gas at $101.3 \text{ kPa}$ and $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The bulb has a maximum pressure rating of $150.0 \text{ kPa}$ before it will shatter. Calculate the maximum temperature the bulb can withstand in degrees Celsius before it breaks.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert initial temperature to Kelvin
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Setup the Equation
$P_1 = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$
$P_2 = 150.0 \text{ kPa}$ (The breaking point)
$$ \frac{101.3}{298.15} = \frac{150.0}{T_2} $$

Step 3: Solve algebraically for $T_2$ (in denominator)
Cross-multiply:
$101.3 \times T_2 = 150.0 \times 298.15$
$101.3 \times T_2 = 44722.5$
$T_2 = \frac{44722.5}{101.3} = 441.485... \text{ K}$

Step 4: Convert back to Celsius
$$ T_2(^\circ\text{C}) = 441.485 - 273.15 = 168.335 \ ^\circ\text{C} $$

Final Answer: The bulb will shatter if the temperature exceeds $\mathbf{168 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Extension

Example 4: Proportional Changes

A chemist wishes to increase the pressure of a rigid reaction vessel by exactly $35.0\%$. If the initial temperature of the vessel was $10.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$, what must the final temperature be (in Celsius) to achieve this pressure increase?

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert $T_1$ to Kelvin
$T_1 = 10.0 + 273.15 = 283.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Express $P_2$ in terms of $P_1$
A $35.0\%$ increase means the final pressure is $135.0\%$ of the original.
$P_2 = 1.35 \times P_1$

Step 3: Apply Amontons' Law
$$ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{P_1}{283.15} = \frac{1.35 \times P_1}{T_2} $$

Step 4: Solve for $T_2$
Divide both sides by $P_1$ to cancel the pressure variable:
$$ \frac{1}{283.15} = \frac{1.35}{T_2} $$ $$ T_2 = 1.35 \times 283.15 = 382.2525 \text{ K} $$

Step 5: Convert to Celsius
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 382.2525 - 273.15 = \mathbf{109.10 \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$

(Notice that while the absolute temperature increased by 35%, the Celsius temperature jumped from 10 to 109—a nearly 1000% increase! This perfectly highlights why using Kelvin is mandatory).

Practise Questions

Apply your understanding of Amontons' Law. Use scrap paper for your working out, then reveal the solution and evaluate yourself using the marking criteria provided.

Question 1: Direct Kelvin Calculation (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A fixed mass of gas in a rigid container exerts a pressure of $1.50 \text{ atm}$ at a temperature of $300 \text{ K}$. If the container is cooled to $200 \text{ K}$, calculate the new pressure of the gas.

Hint: The temperature is already given in Kelvin, so no conversions are needed! Just plug the values into $\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2}$ and solve for $P_2$.

Worked Solution:

$$ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} $$

$$ \frac{1.50}{300} = \frac{P_2}{200} $$

$$ P_2 = \left(\frac{1.50}{300}\right) \times 200 $$

$$ P_2 = 0.005 \times 200 = \mathbf{1.00 \text{ atm}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes variables into Amontons' Law.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final pressure of $1.00 \text{ atm}$.

Question 2: The Celsius Pitfall (Medium)

[3 Marks]

The pressure inside an oxygen tank is $12.0 \text{ atm}$ when left in a room at $20.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. A fire breaks out, heating the tank to $400 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the new pressure inside the tank.

Hint: Step 1: Add $273.15$ to both temperatures to convert them to Kelvin. Step 2: Use the formula to find the final pressure.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert Temperatures:
$T_1 = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 \text{ K}$
$T_2 = 400 + 273.15 = 673.15 \text{ K}$

2. Apply Formula:
$$ \frac{12.0}{293.15} = \frac{P_2}{673.15} $$

3. Solve for $P_2$:
$$ P_2 = \frac{12.0 \times 673.15}{293.15} $$ $$ P_2 = \mathbf{27.55... \text{ atm}} $$ Rounded to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{27.6 \text{ atm}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts BOTH temperatures to Kelvin.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the algebraic fraction to solve for $P_2$.
  • 3 Marks: Correct final pressure ($27.6 \text{ atm}$).

Question 3: Car Tyre Real World Scenario (Hard)

[4 Marks]

On a cold morning at $10.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$, a car's tyre pressure is measured at $220 \text{ kPa}$. After driving at high speeds on the highway, friction heats the air inside the rigid tyre, and the pressure rises to $245 \text{ kPa}$. Calculate the temperature of the air inside the tyre in degrees Celsius.

Hint: Use $\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2}$. Make sure $T_1$ is in Kelvin! Solve for $T_2$ (which is in the denominator, so be careful with the algebra). Finally, convert your answer back to Celsius.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert $T_1$ to Kelvin:
$T_1 = 10.0 + 273.15 = 283.15 \text{ K}$

2. Set up Equation:
$$ \frac{220}{283.15} = \frac{245}{T_2} $$

3. Cross-multiply and solve for $T_2$:
$$ 220 \times T_2 = 245 \times 283.15 $$ $$ 220 \times T_2 = 69371.75 $$ $$ T_2 = 315.326... \text{ K} $$

4. Convert back to Celsius:
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 315.326 - 273.15 = \mathbf{42.176... \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$ Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{42.2 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Converts $10.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $283.15 \text{ K}$.
  • 2 Marks: Substitutes correctly into the formula.
  • 3 Marks: Algebraically isolates $T_2$ correctly to find $\approx 315 \text{ K}$.
  • 4 Marks: Correctly converts the final answer back to Celsius ($42.2 \ ^\circ\text{C}$).
Extension

Question 4: Maximum Pressure Limits (Extension)

[4 Marks]

A steel cylinder contains an ideal gas at $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $101.3 \text{ kPa}$. The engineering specifications state that the cylinder will rupture if the internal pressure exceeds exactly double its current pressure.

Without using the actual pressure numbers in your primary calculation, prove mathematically at what temperature (in Celsius) the cylinder will rupture.

Hint: If the pressure doubles, then $P_2 = 2 P_1$. Substitute this ratio into the formula $\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2}$ and cancel out $P_1$. Then solve for $T_2$.

Worked Solution:

1. Define the relationship:
The rupture pressure $P_2 = 2 \times P_1$.
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$.

2. Substitute into Amontons' Law:
$$ \frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{P_1}{298.15} = \frac{2P_1}{T_2} $$

3. Simplify and solve:
Divide both sides by $P_1$ to eliminate it entirely:
$$ \frac{1}{298.15} = \frac{2}{T_2} $$ $$ T_2 = 2 \times 298.15 = 596.30 \text{ K} $$

4. Convert to Celsius:
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 596.30 - 273.15 = \mathbf{323.15 \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$

(Notice that to double the pressure, the ABSOLUTE temperature must double. $298.15 \times 2 = 596.3$. The Celsius value goes from $25$ to $323$!)

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts $T_1$ to Kelvin ($298.15 \text{ K}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the mathematical proof using $P_2 = 2P_1$.
  • 3 Marks: Concludes that $T_2$ must be exactly double $T_1$ ($596.30 \text{ K}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly converts the final temperature to Celsius ($\approx 323 \ ^\circ\text{C}$).
