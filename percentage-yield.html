15. Percentage Yield

Theoretical Expectations vs. Harsh Chemical Reality

The Myth of 100% Efficiency

Up until this point in the course, we have assumed that chemical reactions are perfect. We assumed that if you mix exactly $2.0 \text{ mol}$ of Hydrogen and $1.0 \text{ mol}$ of Oxygen, you will get exactly $2.0 \text{ mol}$ of Water. In the sterile world of mathematics, this is called the Theoretical Yield.

However, if you step into a laboratory and actually perform the reaction, you will quickly discover that reality is disorganised, messy, and imperfect. The mass of the product you actually collect, dry, and weigh on the electronic balance is called the Actual Yield.

$$ \text{Percentage Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}} \times 100 $$

Why is the Actual Yield almost always lower than the Theoretical Yield?

There are several physical and chemical reasons why you rarely obtain 100% of your expected product. The NSW HSC syllabus expects you to be able to identify and explain these factors:

  • Loss during transfer: When pouring liquids from a beaker to a conical flask, droplets remain stuck to the glass walls.
  • Incomplete precipitation/crystallisation: Some solid product may remain dissolved in the solvent and pass through the filter paper.
  • Side reactions: The reactants might undergo unintended secondary reactions with impurities, oxygen in the air, or the solvent itself, forming products you didn't want.
  • Equilibrium: Some reactions are reversible ($ \rightleftharpoons $). They do not proceed entirely to completion, but rather establish a balance where reactants and products exist simultaneously.
  • Impure Reactants: If you weigh out $10 \text{ g}$ of a reactant, but $1 \text{ g}$ of it is dirt/impurities, you only actually reacted $9 \text{ g}$ of the active chemical.
⚠️
Common Pitfall: Units Matter!
You can calculate percentage yield using either moles or mass (grams). However, you must not mix them. You cannot divide an actual yield in grams by a theoretical yield in moles. Always ensure both numerator and denominator share the exact same units before multiplying by 100.

Worked Solutions

Review these guided examples before attempting the practise questions. Click "View Solution" to reveal the step-by-step mathematical working.

Easy

Direct Substitution

A chemist calculates that $15.5 \text{ g}$ of Aspirin should form during an esterification reaction. However, after filtration and drying, she places the product on a scale and records a mass of $11.2 \text{ g}$. Calculate the percentage yield.

Step 1: Identify given values.
Theoretical Yield = $15.5 \text{ g}$ (What should form)
Actual Yield = $11.2 \text{ g}$ (What actually formed)

Step 2: Apply the formula.
$$ \% \text{ Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual}}{\text{Theoretical}} \times 100 $$ $$ \% \text{ Yield} = \frac{11.2}{15.5} \times 100 $$ $$ \% \text{ Yield} = 72.258... $$

Step 3: Final answer with sig figs.
Given values are to 3 significant figures.
Answer: $72.3\%$

Medium

Stoichiometry to Yield

When $50.0 \text{ g}$ of Silicon Dioxide ($SiO_2$) is heated with excess carbon, $27.9 \text{ g}$ of Silicon Carbide ($SiC$) is produced according to the following equation:
$$ SiO_2(s) + 3C(s) \rightarrow SiC(s) + 2CO(g) $$
Calculate the percentage yield of Silicon Carbide.

To find the % yield, we first need to calculate the Theoretical Yield of SiC using standard mass-to-mass stoichiometry.

Step 1: Moles of known reactant ($SiO_2$).
$MM(SiO_2) = 28.09 + (2 \times 16.00) = 60.09 \text{ g/mol}$
$n(SiO_2) = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{50.0}{60.09} = 0.832 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Mole ratio.
Ratio of $SiO_2 : SiC$ is $1:1$.
Therefore, theoretically, $n(SiC) = 0.832 \text{ mol}$.

Step 3: Calculate Theoretical Mass of $SiC$.
$MM(SiC) = 28.09 + 12.01 = 40.10 \text{ g/mol}$
$m(SiC) = n \times MM = 0.832 \times 40.10 = 33.36 \text{ g}$ (Theoretical Yield)

Step 4: Calculate Percentage Yield.
Actual Yield (given) = $27.9 \text{ g}$
$$ \% \text{ Yield} = \frac{27.9}{33.36} \times 100 = \mathbf{83.6\%} $$

Hard

Deducing Products & Limiting Reagents

$10.0 \text{ g}$ of Lead (II) Nitrate is added to a beaker containing $5.00 \text{ g}$ of Potassium Iodide. A brilliant yellow precipitate forms. The student filters the precipitate, dries it, and weighs it, recording a mass of $6.20 \text{ g}$.

Deduce the balanced chemical equation (with states), determine the theoretical yield of the precipitate, and calculate the percentage yield.

Step 1: Equation & Products.
This is a double displacement precipitation reaction. Lead Iodide is insoluble.
$Pb(NO_3)_2(aq) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s) + 2KNO_3(aq)$

Step 2: Find Limiting Reagent.
$n(Pb(NO_3)_2) = \frac{10.0}{331.2} = 0.0302 \text{ mol}$. Test: $0.0302 / 1 = 0.0302$
$n(KI) = \frac{5.00}{166.0} = 0.0301 \text{ mol}$. Test: $0.0301 / 2 = 0.01505$
KI is the Limiting Reagent.

Step 3: Theoretical Moles of Precipitate ($PbI_2$).
Ratio of $KI : PbI_2$ is $2:1$.
$n(PbI_2) = 0.0301 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.01505 \text{ mol}$.

Step 4: Theoretical Mass of Precipitate.
$MM(PbI_2) = 207.2 + (2 \times 126.9) = 461.0 \text{ g/mol}$.
Theoretical Mass = $0.01505 \times 461.0 = 6.94 \text{ g}$.

Step 5: Percentage Yield.
$$ \% = \frac{6.20}{6.94} \times 100 = \mathbf{89.3\%} $$

Extension

Reverse Calculations (The Factory Dilemma)

An industrial plant needs to synthesise exactly $500 \text{ kg}$ of Ammonia gas ($NH_3$) per day via the Haber process: $N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)$. Due to the reversible nature of the reaction, the plant historically operates at a $42.0\%$ yield.

Calculate the minimum mass of Nitrogen gas ($N_2$) that must be supplied to the reactor to achieve the actual desired yield of $500 \text{ kg}$.

This is a backwards calculation. We have the Actual Yield ($500 \text{ kg}$) and the $\%$, so we must first find the Theoretical Yield.

Step 1: Calculate Theoretical Yield of $NH_3$.
$42.0\% = \frac{500 \text{ kg}}{\text{Theoretical}} \times 100$
$0.420 = \frac{500}{\text{Theoretical}}$
$\text{Theoretical Mass of } NH_3 = \frac{500}{0.420} = 1190.47 \text{ kg} = 1,190,470 \text{ g}$.

Step 2: Moles of $NH_3$ (Theoretical).
$n(NH_3) = \frac{1,190,470}{17.034} = 69,887.8 \text{ mol}$.

Step 3: Mole Ratio to find Nitrogen ($N_2$).
Ratio of $NH_3 : N_2$ is $2:1$.
$n(N_2) = \frac{69,887.8}{2} = 34,943.9 \text{ mol}$.

Step 4: Convert back to Mass.
$MM(N_2) = 28.02 \text{ g/mol}$.
$m(N_2) = 34,943.9 \times 28.02 = 979,128 \text{ g}$.
Converting to kg gives $\mathbf{979 \text{ kg}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Question 1: The Decomposition Yield

[3 Marks]

Calcium Carbonate decomposes upon heating: $CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g)$.
A student heats $25.0 \text{ g}$ of $CaCO_3$ and calculates that $14.0 \text{ g}$ of solid Calcium Oxide ($CaO$) should form. After the experiment, the student weighs the remaining solid and finds it is $11.5 \text{ g}$.

Calculate the percentage yield of $CaO$.

Hint: Read the question carefully. The theoretical yield is already given to you! You do not need to calculate molar masses for this question, just apply the yield formula.
Worked Solution:

$\text{Theoretical Yield} = 14.0 \text{ g}$ (given in question)
$\text{Actual Yield} = 11.5 \text{ g}$ (experimental result)

$\% \text{ Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual}}{\text{Theoretical}} \times 100$
$\% \text{ Yield} = \frac{11.5}{14.0} \times 100$
$\% \text{ Yield} = 82.142...$

Answer: $82.1\%$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies $14.0\text{g}$ as Theoretical and $11.5\text{g}$ as Actual.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the division ($\frac{11.5}{14.0}$).
  • 3 Marks: Calculates final answer correctly ($82.1\%$).

Question 2: Combustion of Magnesium

[4 Marks]

Magnesium burns in oxygen to form Magnesium Oxide: $2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)$.
If $4.50 \text{ g}$ of Magnesium ribbon is completely burned in excess oxygen, and $6.85 \text{ g}$ of Magnesium Oxide powder is collected, calculate the percentage yield.

Hint: Convert $4.50 \text{ g}$ of Mg to moles. Use the ratio $2:2$ (which simplifies to $1:1$) to find the theoretical moles of MgO. Convert that back to grams to find your Theoretical Yield, then divide $6.85$ by it.
Worked Solution:

1. Moles of Mg:
$n(Mg) = \frac{4.50}{24.31} = 0.1851 \text{ mol}$.

2. Theoretical Moles of MgO:
Ratio $Mg : MgO$ is $2:2$ (or $1:1$).
$n(MgO) = 0.1851 \text{ mol}$.

3. Theoretical Mass of MgO:
$MM(MgO) = 24.31 + 16.00 = 40.31 \text{ g/mol}$.
$m(MgO) = 0.1851 \times 40.31 = 7.46 \text{ g}$ (Theoretical).

4. Percentage Yield:
$\% = \frac{6.85}{7.46} \times 100 = \mathbf{91.8\%}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates moles of Magnesium ($0.185 \text{ mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly applies mole ratio to find moles of MgO.
  • 3 Marks: Calculates Theoretical mass of MgO ($7.46 \text{ g}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correct final percentage calculation ($91.8\%$).

Question 3: Synthesis & Deduction

[5 Marks]

A chemist reacts $15.0 \text{ g}$ of pure Aluminium metal with excess Chlorine gas to synthesise Aluminium Chloride. The chemist manages to collect $60.5 \text{ g}$ of product.

Deduce the balanced chemical equation (with state symbols), determine the theoretical yield, and calculate the percentage yield.

Hint: Chlorine gas is diatomic ($Cl_2$). Aluminium has a valency of +3, so Aluminium Chloride is $AlCl_3$. Balance the equation $Al(s) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow AlCl_3(s)$ before doing any maths.
Worked Solution:

1. Equation:
$2Al(s) + 3Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2AlCl_3(s)$

2. Moles of Al:
$n(Al) = \frac{15.0}{26.98} = 0.5560 \text{ mol}$.

3. Theoretical Moles of $AlCl_3$:
Ratio $Al : AlCl_3$ is $2:2$ (or $1:1$).
$n(AlCl_3) = 0.5560 \text{ mol}$.

4. Theoretical Mass of $AlCl_3$:
$MM(AlCl_3) = 26.98 + (3 \times 35.45) = 133.33 \text{ g/mol}$.
$m(AlCl_3) = 0.5560 \times 133.33 = 74.13 \text{ g}$ (Theoretical).

5. Percentage Yield:
$\% = \frac{60.5}{74.13} \times 100 = \mathbf{81.6\%}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correct balanced equation.
  • 2 Marks: Correct state symbols $(s)$ and $(g)$ included.
  • 3 Marks: Correct moles of Al calculated ($0.556 \text{ mol}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correct theoretical mass of $AlCl_3$ ($74.13 \text{ g}$).
  • 5 Marks: Correct final percentage calculation ($81.6\%$).

Question 4: The Impure Limiting Reagent

[6 Marks]

A $20.0 \text{ g}$ sample of Sodium Carbonate is known to be only $85.0\%$ pure (the rest is unreactive dirt). This sample is reacted with $15.0 \text{ g}$ of Hydrochloric acid to produce Sodium Chloride, Water, and Carbon Dioxide gas.

If the experimental setup captures exactly $3.50 \text{ g}$ of $CO_2$ gas, write the balanced equation with states, determine the Limiting Reagent, and calculate the percentage yield of the gas.

Hint: Step 1 is to find the actual mass of active $Na_2CO_3$ by calculating $85\%$ of $20.0 \text{ g}$. Then proceed as a standard Limiting Reagent question.
Worked Solution:

1. Equation:
$Na_2CO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow 2NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)$

2. Pure Mass & Moles:
Pure $Na_2CO_3 = 0.85 \times 20.0 = 17.0 \text{ g}$.
$n(Na_2CO_3) = \frac{17.0}{105.99} = 0.160 \text{ mol}$.
$n(HCl) = \frac{15.0}{36.46} = 0.411 \text{ mol}$.

3. Limiting Reagent Test:
Test $Na_2CO_3: 0.160 / 1 = 0.160$
Test $HCl: 0.411 / 2 = 0.205$
$Na_2CO_3$ is the Limiting Reagent.

4. Theoretical Moles & Mass of $CO_2$:
Ratio $Na_2CO_3 : CO_2$ is $1:1$.
$n(CO_2) = 0.160 \text{ mol}$.
$m(CO_2) = 0.160 \times 44.01 = 7.04 \text{ g}$ (Theoretical Yield).

5. Percentage Yield:
$\% = \frac{3.50}{7.04} \times 100 = \mathbf{49.7\%}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Balanced equation with correct states.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates mass of pure reactant ($17.0 \text{ g}$).
  • 3 Marks: Calculates correct moles for both reactants.
  • 4 Marks: Correctly identifies $Na_2CO_3$ as the Limiting Reagent.
  • 5 Marks: Calculates theoretical mass of $CO_2$ ($7.04 \text{ g}$).
  • 6 Marks: Correct final percentage calculation ($49.7\%$).
