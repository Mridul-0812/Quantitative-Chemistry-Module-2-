10. Mole Ratios

The Heart of Stoichiometry: Bridging the Gap Between Chemicals

The Chemical Recipe

Welcome to Phase C: Stoichiometry. If you only remember one thing from Module 2, let it be this lesson. Stoichiometry is the mathematics of chemistry. It allows us to predict exactly how much product we will make, or how much reactant we need.

Think of a balanced chemical equation like a recipe for baking. If a recipe calls for $2 \text{ eggs}$ and $1 \text{ cup of flour}$ to make $5 \text{ pancakes}$, the ratio is $2:1:5$. If you have $4 \text{ eggs}$, you know instantly you need $2 \text{ cups of flour}$ to make $10 \text{ pancakes}$.

In chemistry, the big numbers at the front of a balanced equation (the coefficients) are your recipe. They tell you the ratio of moles.

The Ultimate Stoichiometry Formula

$$ n(\text{want}) = n(\text{have}) \times \frac{\text{Coefficient of Want}}{\text{Coefficient of Have}} $$

Where "Want" is the substance you are trying to find, and "Have" is the substance you already know the moles of.

We often call this the "Want over Have" rule. It is the bridge that allows you to cross from one chemical to another in an equation.

Worked Examples

Example 1: The 1:1 Ratio (Easy)

Consider the reaction: $HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O$. If you have $0.25 \text{ mol}$ of $HCl$, how many moles of $NaCl$ will be produced?

Step 1: Identify Known and Unknown.

Known (Have) = $HCl$ ($0.25 \text{ mol}$). Unknown (Want) = $NaCl$.

Step 2: Check coefficients from the balanced equation.

Coefficient of $NaCl$ = $1$. Coefficient of $HCl$ = $1$.

Step 3: Apply the bridge formula.

$$ n(NaCl) = n(HCl) \times \frac{1}{1} $$

$$ n(NaCl) = 0.25 \times 1 = \mathbf{0.25 \text{ mol}} $$

Example 2: Fractional Ratios (Medium)

Aluminium reacts with chlorine gas: $2Al + 3Cl_2 \rightarrow 2AlCl_3$. If you want to synthesise $4.50 \text{ mol}$ of $AlCl_3$, how many moles of $Cl_2$ do you need?

Step 1: Identify Known and Unknown.

Known = $AlCl_3$ ($4.50 \text{ mol}$). Unknown = $Cl_2$.

Step 2: Find the ratio (Want / Have).

Want ($Cl_2$) coefficient = $3$. Have ($AlCl_3$) coefficient = $2$. Ratio = $\frac{3}{2}$.

Step 3: Calculate.

$$ n(Cl_2) = n(AlCl_3) \times \frac{3}{2} $$

$$ n(Cl_2) = 4.50 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{6.75 \text{ mol}} $$

Example 3: Working Backwards (Hard)

Potassium chlorate decomposes upon heating: $2KClO_3 \rightarrow 2KCl + 3O_2$. An experiment produces $0.186 \text{ mol}$ of Oxygen gas ($O_2$). How many moles of $KClO_3$ were decomposed to produce this?

Step 1: Known vs Unknown.

Have: $O_2$ ($0.186 \text{ mol}$). Want: $KClO_3$.

Step 2: Ratio (Want/Have).

Want ($KClO_3$) = $2$. Have ($O_2$) = $3$. Ratio = $\frac{2}{3}$.

Step 3: Calculate.

$$ n(KClO_3) = n(O_2) \times \frac{2}{3} $$

$$ n(KClO_3) = 0.186 \times \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{0.124 \text{ mol}} $$

Example 4: Large Coefficients (Extension)

The combustion of octane: $2C_8H_{18} + 25O_2 \rightarrow 16CO_2 + 18H_2O$. If $12.5 \text{ mol}$ of octane undergoes complete combustion, calculate the total moles of gas produced (assuming water is a liquid, not a gas, at room temp).

Step 1: Identify the "gas produced".

The only gas produced (if water is liquid) is $CO_2$. So we want moles of $CO_2$.

Have: $C_8H_{18}$ ($12.5 \text{ mol}$).

Step 2: Ratio.

Want ($CO_2$) = $16$. Have ($C_8H_{18}$) = $2$. Ratio = $\frac{16}{2} = 8$.

Step 3: Calculate.

$$ n(CO_2) = 12.5 \times 8 = \mathbf{100 \text{ mol}} $$

(Note: If the question asked for total moles of ALL products, you would do a separate calculation for water and add them up!)

Practice Questions

Question 1: Easy

[1 Mark]

Given the equation: $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$. If you react $4.0 \text{ mol}$ of Nitrogen ($N_2$) completely, how many moles of Ammonia ($NH_3$) will form?

Hint: Use $\frac{\text{Want}}{\text{Have}}$. You want $NH_3$ (coeff 2), you have $N_2$ (coeff 1).

Worked Solution:

$n(NH_3) = n(N_2) \times \frac{2}{1}$

$n(NH_3) = 4.0 \times 2 = \mathbf{8.0 \text{ mol}}$

  • 1 Mark: Correct final answer of $8.0 \text{ mol}$.

Question 2: Medium

[2 Marks]

Consider the reaction: $4P + 5O_2 \rightarrow 2P_2O_5$. How many moles of Oxygen gas ($O_2$) are required to fully react with $0.60 \text{ mol}$ of Phosphorus ($P$)?

Hint: The fraction is $\frac{5}{4}$. Multiply $0.60$ by this fraction.

Worked Solution:

$n(O_2) = n(P) \times \frac{5}{4}$

$n(O_2) = 0.60 \times 1.25 = \mathbf{0.75 \text{ mol}}$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly establishes the molar ratio as $5/4$.
  • 1 Mark: Correct final calculation of $0.75 \text{ mol}$.

Question 3: Hard

[2 Marks]

A blast furnace reduces iron ore: $Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2$. If $450 \text{ mol}$ of Iron ($Fe$) is collected, how many moles of Carbon Monoxide ($CO$) were consumed in the process?

Hint: You are starting from the product side and working backwards to the reactant. Have = $Fe$, Want = $CO$.

Worked Solution:

Want: $CO$ (coeff 3). Have: $Fe$ (coeff 2). Ratio: $\frac{3}{2}$.

$n(CO) = n(Fe) \times \frac{3}{2}$

$n(CO) = 450 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{675 \text{ mol}}$

  • 1 Mark: Uses the inverted ratio correctly ($\frac{3}{2}$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct final answer of $675 \text{ mol}$.

Question 4: Extension

[3 Marks]

Baking soda decomposes under heat: $2NaHCO_3 \rightarrow Na_2CO_3 + H_2O + CO_2$. In a secondary process, the $CO_2$ produced is immediately reacted with calcium hydroxide: $CO_2 + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3 + H_2O$. If you start with $5.0 \text{ mol}$ of $NaHCO_3$, how many moles of $CaCO_3$ will eventually be produced?

Hint: This is a two-step bridge. First, find moles of $CO_2$ produced from the $NaHCO_3$. Then use that mole value as the "Have" for the second equation.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Moles of $CO_2$ generated.

$n(CO_2) = n(NaHCO_3) \times \frac{1}{2} = 5.0 \times 0.5 = 2.5 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Moles of $CaCO_3$ produced.

From the second equation, ratio of $CaCO_3$ to $CO_2$ is $1:1$.

$n(CaCO_3) = n(CO_2) \times \frac{1}{1} = 2.5 \times 1 = \mathbf{2.5 \text{ mol}}$

  • 1 Mark: Recognises the two-step stoichiometric process.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates $2.5 \text{ mol}$ of intermediate $CO_2$.
  • 1 Mark: Correctly bridges $1:1$ in the second equation for the final answer.
