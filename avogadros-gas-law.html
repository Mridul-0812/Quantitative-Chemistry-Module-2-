24. Avogadro's Gas Law

The Mathematical Relationship Between Volume and Moles

The Law of Equal Volumes

We have examined how Pressure, Volume, and Temperature interact. But what happens if we actively pump more gas into a flexible container? In 1811, Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro proposed a revolutionary hypothesis that became a cornerstone of modern stoichiometry.

Avogadro's Law Definition

For a gas held at a strictly constant temperature ($T$) and constant pressure ($P$), the volume ($V$) of the gas is directly proportional to the number of moles ($n$) of the gas.

Furthermore, Avogadro stated that equal volumes of ANY ideal gas, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the exact same number of molecules (moles), regardless of the size or mass of the individual gas particles.

[Image illustrating Avogadro's law: three identical balloons containing different gases (He, O2, CO2) but all having the same volume, indicating they contain the same number of moles]

Mathematical Derivation

Since Volume ($V$) is directly proportional to Moles ($n$), we write:

$$ V \propto n $$

Introducing a constant of proportionality ($k$):

$$ V = k \times n \quad \implies \quad \frac{V}{n} = k $$

Because dividing the Volume by the Moles always yields a constant value ($k$) under fixed conditions, we can equate two different states of a gas system:

$$ \frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2} $$

The Future Link: The Ideal Gas Law

As with the previous laws, we can derive Avogadro's Law directly from the Ideal Gas Law ($PV = nRT$).

If we rearrange $PV = nRT$ to group our changing variables ($V$ and $n$) on one side, and our constants on the other, we get:

$$ \frac{V}{n} = \frac{RT}{P} $$

  • $P$ is the Pressure (constant).
  • $T$ is the Temperature (constant).
  • $R$ is the Universal Gas Constant ($8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$).

Since the entire right side of the equation consists of constants, the term $\frac{RT}{P}$ evaluates to a single constant number ($k$). Therefore, $\frac{V}{n} = k$, proving Avogadro's Law perfectly!

Worked Solutions

Study how the formula $\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2}$ is applied in these HSC-style worked examples. Pay attention to how mass must often be converted to moles first.

Easy

Example 1: Direct Molar Substitution

A weather balloon containing $2.50 \text{ mol}$ of Helium gas occupies a volume of $60.0 \text{ L}$. A technician pumps an additional $1.50 \text{ mol}$ of Helium into the balloon. Assuming pressure and temperature remain constant, calculate the final volume of the balloon.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Extract the data
$V_1 = 60.0 \text{ L}$
$n_1 = 2.50 \text{ mol}$
$V_2 = \text{ ?}$
$n_2 = 2.50 \text{ mol (initial)} + 1.50 \text{ mol (added)} = 4.00 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Apply Avogadro's Law
$$ \frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2} $$ $$ \frac{60.0}{2.50} = \frac{V_2}{4.00} $$

Step 3: Rearrange and solve
Multiply both sides by $4.00$ to isolate $V_2$:
$$ V_2 = \left(\frac{60.0}{2.50}\right) \times 4.00 $$ $$ V_2 = 24.0 \times 4.00 = 96.0 \text{ L} $$

Final Answer: The final volume of the balloon is $\mathbf{96.0 \text{ L}}$.

Medium

Example 2: Mass to Moles Conversion

A cylinder fitted with a movable piston contains $32.0 \text{ g}$ of Oxygen gas ($O_2$), occupying a volume of $24.5 \text{ L}$. How many grams of Oxygen gas must be released from the cylinder to reduce the volume to $15.0 \text{ L}$ at constant temperature and pressure?

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert Initial Mass to Moles
$MM(O_2) = 2 \times 16.00 = 32.00 \text{ g/mol}$
$n_1 = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{32.0}{32.00} = 1.00 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Setup the Equation
$V_1 = 24.5 \text{ L}$
$n_1 = 1.00 \text{ mol}$
$V_2 = 15.0 \text{ L}$
$n_2 = \text{ ?}$
$$ \frac{24.5}{1.00} = \frac{15.0}{n_2} $$

Step 3: Solve for $n_2$
Cross-multiply:
$24.5 \times n_2 = 15.0 \times 1.00$
$n_2 = \frac{15.0}{24.5} = 0.6122... \text{ mol}$ (This is the final moles required).

Step 4: Find the Moles Released
$\text{Moles released} = n_1 - n_2$
$\text{Moles released} = 1.00 - 0.6122 = 0.3877... \text{ mol}$

Step 5: Convert Released Moles to Mass
$m = n \times MM = 0.3877 \times 32.00 = \mathbf{12.4 \text{ g}}$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{12.4 \text{ g}}$ of $O_2$ must be released (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Comparing Different Gases

At a specific temperature and pressure, $4.00 \text{ g}$ of Helium gas ($He$) occupies $25.0 \text{ L}$. Under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, what mass of Carbon Dioxide gas ($CO_2$) is required to occupy a volume of $75.0 \text{ L}$?

Worked Solution:

Avogadro's Law states that equal volumes of ANY ideal gas under the same conditions contain the same number of moles. Therefore, we can use the data from Helium to find the proportional constant for Carbon Dioxide.

Step 1: Calculate moles of Helium
$MM(He) = 4.003 \text{ g/mol}$
$n(He) = \frac{4.00}{4.003} = 0.99925 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Apply Avogadro's Ratio
Let state 1 be Helium, state 2 be Carbon Dioxide.
$$ \frac{V_{He}}{n_{He}} = \frac{V_{CO_2}}{n_{CO_2}} $$ $$ \frac{25.0}{0.99925} = \frac{75.0}{n_{CO_2}} $$

Step 3: Solve for moles of $CO_2$
$$ n_{CO_2} = \frac{75.0 \times 0.99925}{25.0} = 2.99775 \text{ mol} $$

Step 4: Convert moles of $CO_2$ to mass
$MM(CO_2) = 12.01 + (2 \times 16.00) = 44.01 \text{ g/mol}$
$m = n \times MM = 2.99775 \times 44.01 = \mathbf{131.93 \text{ g}}$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{132 \text{ g}}$ of $CO_2$ is required (to 3 sig figs).

Extension

Example 4: Reaction Stoichiometry Integration

Methane ($CH_4$) combusts in Oxygen ($O_2$) according to the equation:
$CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)$

If $5.00 \text{ L}$ of Methane completely reacts with $10.0 \text{ L}$ of Oxygen at constant temperature and pressure, calculate the final volume of the gas mixture remaining after the reaction. (Assume the water formed is completely liquid with negligible volume).

Worked Solution:

Because Volume is directly proportional to Moles (Avogadro's Law), we can use the stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation directly as volume ratios!

Step 1: Check Reactants
Equation ratio $CH_4 : O_2$ is $1 : 2$.
We have $5.00 \text{ L}$ of $CH_4$ and $10.0 \text{ L}$ of $O_2$. This is exactly a $1:2$ ratio, so both reactants will be completely consumed.

Step 2: Determine Products
The products are $1 \text{ vol } CO_2(g)$ and $2 \text{ vol } H_2O(l)$.
Because the water is liquid, it contributes approximately $0 \text{ L}$ to the final gas volume.
We only need to calculate the volume of $CO_2$ produced.

Step 3: Apply Volume Ratios
Ratio of $CH_4(g) : CO_2(g)$ is $1 : 1$.
Therefore, if $5.00 \text{ L}$ of $CH_4$ reacts, exactly $5.00 \text{ L}$ of $CO_2$ gas will be produced.

Final Answer: The final volume of the gas mixture is $\mathbf{5.00 \text{ L}}$ (consisting entirely of Carbon Dioxide).

Question 1: Direct Substitution (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A $4.50 \text{ L}$ container holds $0.250 \text{ mol}$ of Nitrogen gas. If the container expands to hold $0.800 \text{ mol}$ of Nitrogen gas at the same temperature and pressure, calculate the new volume.

Hint: Setup the ratio $\frac{4.50}{0.250} = \frac{V_2}{0.800}$. Multiply both sides by $0.800$ to solve for $V_2$.

Worked Solution:

$$ \frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2} $$

$$ \frac{4.50}{0.250} = \frac{V_2}{0.800} $$

$$ V_2 = \left(\frac{4.50}{0.250}\right) \times 0.800 $$

$$ V_2 = 18 \times 0.800 = \mathbf{14.4 \text{ L}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes variables into Avogadro's Law.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final volume of $14.4 \text{ L}$.

Question 2: Finding Gas Released (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A balloon contains $1.20 \text{ mol}$ of gas and has a volume of $30.0 \text{ L}$. Gas is slowly released from the balloon until its volume drops to $18.0 \text{ L}$. Calculate the number of moles of gas that were removed from the balloon.

Hint: Step 1: Use the formula to find the final number of moles ($n_2$) remaining in the balloon. Step 2: Subtract this final amount from the initial $1.20 \text{ mol}$ to find how much was removed.

Worked Solution:

1. Find Final Moles ($n_2$):
$$ \frac{30.0}{1.20} = \frac{18.0}{n_2} $$ $$ 30.0 \times n_2 = 18.0 \times 1.20 $$ $$ n_2 = \frac{21.6}{30.0} = 0.720 \text{ mol} $$

2. Find Moles Removed:
$\text{Removed} = n_1 - n_2$
$\text{Removed} = 1.20 - 0.720 = \mathbf{0.480 \text{ mol}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly sets up the algebraic fraction to solve for $n_2$.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates the final moles inside the balloon ($0.720 \text{ mol}$).
  • 3 Marks: Correctly subtracts to find the moles removed ($0.48 \text{ mol}$).

Question 3: Mass Identity (Hard)

[4 Marks]

At a constant temperature and pressure, $2.50 \text{ g}$ of Hydrogen gas ($H_2$) occupies a volume of $31.0 \text{ L}$. An unknown gas under the exact same conditions requires $55.0 \text{ g}$ to occupy the same $31.0 \text{ L}$ volume. Calculate the Molar Mass of the unknown gas.

Hint: Avogadro's law states that equal volumes of gases contain equal moles. If the volume of the unknown gas is the same as the Hydrogen ($31.0 \text{ L}$), it must contain the exact same number of moles. Calculate the moles of $H_2$, then use that mole value and the $55.0 \text{ g}$ mass to find the Molar Mass ($MM = m/n$).

Worked Solution:

1. Calculate moles of Hydrogen ($H_2$):
$MM(H_2) = 2 \times 1.008 = 2.016 \text{ g/mol}$
$n(H_2) = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{2.50}{2.016} = 1.240... \text{ mol}$

2. Apply Avogadro's Principle:
Since both gases occupy $31.0 \text{ L}$ at the same conditions, they must contain the same number of moles.
$n(\text{unknown}) = 1.240... \text{ mol}$

3. Calculate Molar Mass:
$$ MM = \frac{m}{n} $$ $$ MM = \frac{55.0}{1.240...} = 44.35... \text{ g/mol} $$ Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{44.4 \text{ g/mol}}$ (This happens to be the molar mass of Nitrous Oxide, $N_2O$, or Carbon Dioxide, $CO_2$!)

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates the moles of Hydrogen ($1.24 \text{ mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Explicitly states or mathematically demonstrates that the moles of the unknown gas must equal the moles of Hydrogen.
  • 3 Marks: Uses the rearranged mole formula ($MM = m/n$) correctly.
  • 4 Marks: Correct final Molar Mass ($\approx 44.4 \text{ g/mol}$).
Extension

Question 4: Volume Stoichiometry (Extension)

[4 Marks]

Nitrogen gas ($N_2$) reacts with Hydrogen gas ($H_2$) to produce Ammonia gas ($NH_3$) according to the Haber process. Write the balanced equation.

If $15.0 \text{ L}$ of Nitrogen gas is reacted with an excess of Hydrogen gas at constant temperature and pressure, calculate the final volume of Ammonia gas produced.

Hint: Balance the equation $N_2 + H_2 \rightarrow NH_3$ first. Because the conditions are constant, Avogadro's Law allows you to treat the stoichiometric coefficients directly as a "Volume Ratio", just like a mole ratio!

Worked Solution:

1. Balanced Equation:
$N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)$

2. Apply Volume Ratios (Avogadro's Law):
Because $V \propto n$, the mole ratio of $1 : 3 : 2$ is identical to the volume ratio.
Ratio of $N_2 : NH_3$ is $1 : 2$.

3. Calculate Volume:
$$ V(NH_3) = V(N_2) \times \frac{2}{1} $$ $$ V(NH_3) = 15.0 \text{ L} \times 2 = \mathbf{30.0 \text{ L}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly balances the equation ($1N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$).
  • 2 Marks: Identifies the ratio between Nitrogen and Ammonia is $1:2$.
  • 3 Marks: Applies Avogadro's concept that mole ratios can be used directly as volume ratios.
  • 4 Marks: Correct final volume ($30.0 \text{ L}$).
