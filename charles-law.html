22. Charles' Law

The Mathematical Relationship Between Volume and Temperature

The Expansion of Gases

In the previous lesson, we saw how Pressure and Volume interact while keeping temperature constant (Boyle's Law). Now, we will investigate what happens when we hold the Pressure constant and change the Temperature. This relationship, formulated by French physicist Jacques Charles in the 1780s, mathematically defines exactly how a gas expands when heated.

Charles' Law Definition

For a fixed mass of gas (constant moles, $n$) held at a strictly constant pressure ($P$), the volume ($V$) of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature ($T$) in Kelvin.

According to Kinetic Molecular Theory, heating a gas increases the kinetic energy (speed) of its particles. Because they move faster, they hit the walls of the container harder and more frequently. To keep the pressure constant, the container must expand to increase the surface area, thus accommodating the extra force. Therefore, heating a gas causes its volume to increase.

Mathematical Derivation

Since Volume ($V$) is directly proportional to Temperature ($T$), we can write this relationship as:

$$ V \propto T $$

By introducing a mathematical constant of proportionality ($k$), we convert this into an equation:

$$ V = k \times T \quad \implies \quad \frac{V}{T} = k $$

This means that for any specific gas at a constant pressure, dividing its Volume by its absolute Temperature will always yield the same constant number ($k$). Therefore, if we change the state of the gas from State 1 to State 2, the ratio of $\frac{V}{T}$ must remain identical.

$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} $$

The Future Link: The Ideal Gas Law

Just like with Boyle's Law, you might wonder what the constant $k$ actually represents. We are building towards the master equation of all gases: The Ideal Gas Law ($PV = nRT$).

If we rearrange $PV = nRT$ to isolate $\frac{V}{T}$ on one side, we get:

$$ \frac{V}{T} = \frac{nR}{P} $$

  • $n$ is the number of moles (which is kept constant in Charles' Law).
  • $P$ is the Pressure (which is kept constant in Charles' Law).
  • $R$ is the Universal Gas Constant. On your NESA Reference Sheet, this is given precisely as $8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$.

Because $n$, $R$, and $P$ are all constants in this scenario, the entire term $\frac{nR}{P}$ is a constant. Hence, $\frac{V}{T} = k$. Understanding how these individual laws derive from $PV=nRT$ is the secret to mastering Phase E!

Worked Solutions

Study these exemplary worked solutions. The absolute most important rule in Charles' Law calculations is that Temperature MUST be in Kelvin. If you use Celsius in the fraction $\frac{V_1}{T_1}$, your answer will be completely wrong.

Easy

Example 1: Direct Substitution

A balloon is filled with $2.50 \text{ L}$ of gas at a temperature of $300 \text{ K}$. The balloon is then taken outside on a hot day where the temperature is $350 \text{ K}$. Assuming the atmospheric pressure remains constant, calculate the new volume of the balloon.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Extract the data
$V_1 = 2.50 \text{ L}$
$T_1 = 300 \text{ K}$
$V_2 = \text{ ?}$
$T_2 = 350 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Apply Charles' Law
$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{2.50}{300} = \frac{V_2}{350} $$

Step 3: Rearrange and solve
Multiply both sides by $350$ to isolate $V_2$:
$$ V_2 = \left(\frac{2.50}{300}\right) \times 350 $$ $$ V_2 = 0.008333... \times 350 $$ $$ V_2 = 2.91666... \text{ L} $$

Final Answer: The new volume is $\mathbf{2.92 \text{ L}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Medium

Example 2: The Celsius Trap

A gas occupies a volume of $450 \text{ mL}$ at $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The gas is heated at constant pressure until its volume reaches $600 \text{ mL}$. Calculate the new temperature of the gas in Kelvin.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert Temperature to Kelvin!
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Extract the data
$V_1 = 450 \text{ mL}$
$T_1 = 298.15 \text{ K}$
$V_2 = 600 \text{ mL}$
$T_2 = \text{ ?}$

(Note: We can leave V in mL as long as both V1 and V2 are in mL).

Step 3: Apply Charles' Law
$$ \frac{450}{298.15} = \frac{600}{T_2} $$

Step 4: Rearrange to solve for the denominator ($T_2$)
Cross-multiply or flip both fractions:
$$ \frac{298.15}{450} = \frac{T_2}{600} $$ $$ T_2 = \left(\frac{298.15}{450}\right) \times 600 $$ $$ T_2 = 397.533... \text{ K} $$

Final Answer: The new temperature is $\mathbf{398 \text{ K}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Double Conversion (Finding Celsius)

A sealed, flexible container holding $5.00 \text{ L}$ of gas at $150 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ is placed into a freezer. The volume of the container shrinks to $3.20 \text{ L}$. Assuming constant pressure, calculate the final temperature of the freezer in degrees Celsius.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert $T_1$ to Kelvin
$T_1 = 150 + 273.15 = 423.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Apply Charles' Law
$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{5.00}{423.15} = \frac{3.20}{T_2} $$

Step 3: Solve for $T_2$ in Kelvin
$$ T_2 = \frac{3.20 \times 423.15}{5.00} $$ $$ T_2 = 270.816 \text{ K} $$

Step 4: Convert back to Celsius
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = T(K) - 273.15 $$ $$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 270.816 - 273.15 = -2.334 \ ^\circ\text{C} $$

Final Answer: The temperature of the freezer is $\mathbf{-2.33 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$.

Extension

Example 4: Proportional/Percentage Increases

A sample of gas is heated at constant pressure such that its absolute temperature (in Kelvin) increases by $40.0\%$. If the original volume of the gas was $V_1$, prove mathematically what the final volume ($V_2$) will be in terms of $V_1$.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Express $T_2$ in terms of $T_1$
An increase of $40.0\%$ means the new temperature is $140.0\%$ of the original.
$T_2 = 1.40 \times T_1$

Step 2: Setup Charles' Law
$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} $$ Substitute $T_2$ into the equation:
$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{1.40 T_1} $$

Step 3: Solve for $V_2$
Multiply both sides by $1.40 T_1$:
$$ V_2 = \frac{V_1 \times 1.40 T_1}{T_1} $$ The $T_1$ variables cancel out completely:
$$ V_2 = 1.40 V_1 $$

Final Answer: The final volume $V_2$ will be exactly $1.40$ times $V_1$. This demonstrates that because Volume and Temperature are directly proportional, a $40\%$ increase in absolute temperature results in exactly a $40\%$ increase in volume!

Question 1: Direct Calculation (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A perfectly elastic balloon contains $3.00 \text{ L}$ of gas at an absolute temperature of $280 \text{ K}$. The balloon is gently heated to $350 \text{ K}$ while maintaining a constant pressure. Calculate the final volume of the balloon.

Hint: Use $\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}$. The temperature is already in Kelvin, so you can plug the numbers straight in! Solve for $V_2$.

Worked Solution:

$$ \frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} $$

$$ \frac{3.00}{280} = \frac{V_2}{350} $$

$$ V_2 = \left(\frac{3.00}{280}\right) \times 350 $$

$$ V_2 = \mathbf{3.75 \text{ L}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes variables into Charles' Law formula.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final volume of $3.75 \text{ L}$.

Question 2: Finding Temperature (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A gas inside a cylinder occupies $500 \text{ mL}$ at a temperature of $22.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The gas expands at constant pressure until its volume reaches $850 \text{ mL}$. Calculate the new temperature of the gas in Kelvin.

Hint: Convert $22.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin first! Then setup the ratio $\frac{500}{T_1} = \frac{850}{T_2}$ and solve for the denominator $T_2$.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert Temperature:
$T_1 = 22.0 + 273.15 = 295.15 \text{ K}$

2. Apply Charles' Law:
$$ \frac{500}{295.15} = \frac{850}{T_2} $$

3. Solve for $T_2$:
$$ T_2 = \frac{850 \times 295.15}{500} $$ $$ T_2 = \mathbf{501.7... \text{ K}} $$ Rounded to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{502 \text{ K}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts initial temperature to Kelvin ($295.15 \text{ K}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the algebraic equation to solve for $T_2$.
  • 3 Marks: Correct final temperature in Kelvin ($\approx 502 \text{ K}$).

Question 3: Returning to Celsius (Hard)

[4 Marks]

A $1.50 \text{ L}$ sample of Argon gas is held at $120 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The gas is cooled at constant pressure until its volume decreases to $0.800 \text{ L}$. Calculate the final temperature of the gas in degrees Celsius.

Hint: Step 1: Celsius to Kelvin. Step 2: Use Charles' Law to find the new temperature in Kelvin. Step 3: Subtract $273.15$ to convert your answer back to Celsius.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert $T_1$ to Kelvin:
$T_1 = 120 + 273.15 = 393.15 \text{ K}$

2. Apply Formula:
$$ \frac{1.50}{393.15} = \frac{0.800}{T_2} $$ $$ T_2 = \frac{0.800 \times 393.15}{1.50} $$ $$ T_2 = 209.68 \text{ K} $$

3. Convert back to Celsius:
$$ T_2(^\circ\text{C}) = 209.68 - 273.15 = \mathbf{-63.5 \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts initial temperature to Kelvin ($393.15 \text{ K}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up Charles' Law.
  • 3 Marks: Correctly calculates final temperature in Kelvin ($209.68 \text{ K}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly converts back to Celsius ($-63.5 \ ^\circ\text{C}$).

Question 4: Temperature Doubling Trap (Extension)

[3 Marks]

A student has $2.0 \text{ L}$ of gas at $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. They heat the gas until the temperature reaches $50.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The student states: "Because the temperature has doubled, Charles' Law dictates the volume will also double to 4.0 L."

Evaluate the accuracy of the student's statement and calculate the true final volume.

Hint: Did the absolute temperature (Kelvin) actually double? Convert $25 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $50 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin and check!

Worked Solution:

Evaluation: The student's statement is incorrect. Charles' Law states that volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature (Kelvin), not Celsius. While the Celsius value doubled, the absolute temperature did not.

Calculation:
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$
$T_2 = 50.0 + 273.15 = 323.15 \text{ K}$

$$ \frac{2.0}{298.15} = \frac{V_2}{323.15} $$ $$ V_2 = \frac{2.0 \times 323.15}{298.15} = \mathbf{2.17 \text{ L}} $$

The true volume only increased slightly, to $2.17 \text{ L}$, not $4.0 \text{ L}$.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies the student is incorrect because volume is proportional to Kelvin, not Celsius.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly converts both temperatures to Kelvin ($298.15 \text{ K}$ and $323.15 \text{ K}$).
  • 3 Marks: Calculates the true final volume correctly ($2.17 \text{ L}$).
