19. Standard Solutions

Volumetric Flasks, Primary Standards & Practical Methodology

The Need for Absolute Precision

In quantitative chemistry, particularly during titrations (which you will explore heavily in Year 12), you must know the exact concentration of the solutions you are using. A solution whose concentration is known to a high degree of accuracy is called a Standard Solution.

Primary vs. Secondary Standards

You cannot simply weigh out any chemical, dissolve it in water, and trust its concentration. Some chemicals absorb moisture from the air, meaning the "mass" you weigh includes water, throwing off your mole calculations. We must categorise standards:

  • Primary Standard: A substance of such high purity and stability that a standard solution can be prepared directly by weighing it and dissolving it to a known volume.
  • Secondary Standard: A solution whose concentration is determined after preparation by titrating it against a known primary standard.

Characteristics of a Good Primary Standard

For a chemical to be deemed a "Primary Standard", it must satisfy several strict criteria. If it fails even one, it cannot be trusted for direct precision weighing.

  • High Level of Purity: Must be free from impurities ($>99.9\%$).
  • High Stability: Must not react with gases in the air (e.g., $O_2$ or $CO_2$) over time.
  • Not Hygroscopic: Must not absorb water vapour from the atmosphere.
  • Not Deliquescent: Must not absorb so much water from the air that it dissolves into a liquid.
  • Not Efflorescent: Must not lose its water of crystallisation to the atmosphere.
  • High Molar Mass: A higher molar mass reduces the percentage error associated with weighing on an electronic balance.

Click on the chemicals below to evaluate if they make a good or bad primary standard. This is highly examinable in the HSC!

Sodium Hydroxide ($NaOH$)

A very common strong base.

Terrible Primary Standard

Highly hygroscopic and deliquescent. It absorbs water from the air so rapidly that its mass increases while sitting on the electronic balance. It also reacts with atmospheric $CO_2$. Must be standardised first!

Anhydrous Sodium Carbonate ($Na_2CO_3$)

A white powdery weak base.

Excellent Primary Standard

It is obtainable in a very pure state, does not absorb moisture readily if anhydrous, is stable, and has a relatively high molar mass ($105.99 \text{ g/mol}$). A staple of school laboratories.

Hydrochloric Acid ($HCl$)

A very common strong acid.

Terrible Primary Standard

Pure $HCl$ is a gas at room temperature. The aqueous solution is volatile, meaning $HCl$ gas escapes from the solution over time, causing the concentration to continually decrease. Must be standardised first!

Oxalic Acid Dihydrate ($H_2C_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O$)

A crystalline organic acid.

Excellent Primary Standard

Highly stable, extremely pure, and non-hygroscopic. It has a high molar mass ($126.07 \text{ g/mol}$) which minimises percentage weighing errors.

The Preparation Procedure

HSC Chemistry questions frequently ask you to "outline the procedure" for preparing a standard solution. Memorise these exact steps, paying close attention to the washing technique.

  1. Weighing: Accurately weigh the calculated mass of the primary standard in a clean, dry beaker on an electronic balance.
  2. Dissolving: Add a small amount of distilled water (e.g., $50 \text{ mL}$) to the beaker and stir with a glass rod until the solid is completely dissolved.
    Do not add too much water yet!
  3. Transferring: Pour the solution into a clean volumetric flask using a clean glass funnel to prevent spillage.
  4. Rinsing (CRUCIAL): Rinse the beaker, the glass rod, and the funnel several times with distilled water, pouring all the washings into the volumetric flask.
    This ensures every single mole of solute makes it into the flask.
  5. Making to the Mark: Add distilled water to the volumetric flask until the bottom of the meniscus rests exactly on the etched calibration line. Use a pasteur pipette for the final few drops.
  6. Inverting: Stopper the flask securely and invert it several times to ensure the solution is completely homogeneous.

Worked Solutions

Review these examples before attempting the practise questions. Pay close attention to how errors impact the final calculated concentration.

Example 1: Mass Required (Easy)

A student needs to prepare $250.0 \text{ mL}$ of a $0.100 \text{ M}$ standard solution of anhydrous Sodium Carbonate ($Na_2CO_3$). Calculate the mass of the solid required.

Step 1: Calculate the moles required.
$V = 250.0 \text{ mL} = 0.2500 \text{ L}$
$n = c \times V$
$n = 0.100 \text{ M} \times 0.2500 \text{ L} = 0.0250 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Calculate the mass required.
$MM(Na_2CO_3) = (2 \times 22.99) + 12.01 + (3 \times 16.00) = 105.99 \text{ g/mol}$
$m = n \times MM$
$m = 0.0250 \times 105.99 = \mathbf{2.65 \text{ g}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Example 2: Determining Concentration (Medium)

A chemist accurately weighs $12.604 \text{ g}$ of primary standard Oxalic Acid Dihydrate ($H_2C_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O$). It is dissolved in water and transferred to a $500.0 \text{ mL}$ volumetric flask. Water is added to the mark. Calculate the precise molarity of the standard solution.

Step 1: Calculate the Molar Mass (including hydration water!).
$MM = (2 \times 1.008) + (2 \times 12.01) + (4 \times 16.00) + 2(18.016)$
$MM = 126.068 \text{ g/mol}$

Step 2: Calculate the Moles.
$n = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{12.604}{126.068} = 0.099977... \text{ mol}$

Step 3: Calculate the Concentration.
$V = 0.5000 \text{ L}$
$c = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.099977}{0.5000} = \mathbf{0.2000 \text{ M}}$ (to 4 sig figs based on data).

Example 3: Error Analysis - Overshooting (Hard)

While preparing a $0.100 \text{ M}$ standard solution of $Na_2CO_3$ in a $250.0 \text{ mL}$ volumetric flask, a student accidentally adds too much distilled water. The bottom of the meniscus sits approximately $2 \text{ mL}$ above the calibration line. State whether the actual concentration is higher, lower, or equal to $0.100 \text{ M}$, and justify your answer.

Answer: The actual concentration is lower than $0.100 \text{ M}$.

Justification: The mass (and therefore moles, $n$) of the solute added to the flask remained constant. However, by overshooting the mark, the total volume of the solution ($V$) has increased. According to the formula $c = \frac{n}{V}$, if the denominator ($V$) increases while the numerator ($n$) remains constant, the resulting concentration ($c$) must decrease.

Example 4: Error Analysis - The Wet Flask (Extension)

A student washes their $250.0 \text{ mL}$ volumetric flask with distilled water before use, but does not dry it. Several droplets of distilled water remain inside before they transfer their dissolved primary standard into it and make it up to the mark. Explain how this will affect the final concentration of the standard solution.

Answer: It will have no effect on the final concentration.

Justification: The volumetric flask is designed to hold exactly $250.0 \text{ mL}$ when filled to the mark. The student will still add distilled water until the meniscus hits that calibration mark. The droplets already inside simply mean the student will have to add slightly less water from their wash bottle to reach the mark. The total moles ($n$) and the total final volume ($V$) remain perfectly correct, so $c$ remains perfectly accurate.

Practise Questions

Question 1: Easy Calculation

[3 Marks]

What mass of solid Sodium Carbonate ($Na_2CO_3$) is required to prepare $500.0 \text{ mL}$ of a $0.0500 \text{ M}$ standard solution?

Hint: Step 1: Calculate the moles needed using $n = c \times V$ (remember to convert mL to L). Step 2: Multiply by the Molar Mass of $Na_2CO_3$ to find the mass.

Worked Solution:

$n = c \times V = 0.0500 \times 0.5000 = 0.0250 \text{ mol}$

$MM(Na_2CO_3) = (2 \times 22.99) + 12.01 + (3 \times 16.00) = 105.99 \text{ g/mol}$

$m = n \times MM = 0.0250 \times 105.99 = \mathbf{2.65 \text{ g}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates required moles ($0.0250 \text{ mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates Molar Mass ($105.99 \text{ g/mol}$).
  • 3 Marks: Correct final mass calculated with correct sig figs ($2.65 \text{ g}$).

Question 2: Finding Concentration (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A $1.520 \text{ g}$ sample of pure Oxalic Acid Dihydrate ($H_2C_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O$) is dissolved and made up to $250.0 \text{ mL}$ in a volumetric flask. Calculate the precise molarity of the solution.

Hint: Do not forget to include the mass of the $2 \times H_2O$ molecules when calculating the molar mass of the solid. Then divide the moles by the volume in Litres.

Worked Solution:

$MM = (2 \times 1.008) + (2 \times 12.01) + (4 \times 16.00) + 2(18.016) = 126.068 \text{ g/mol}$

$n = \frac{m}{MM} = \frac{1.520}{126.068} = 0.0120569... \text{ mol}$

$c = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.0120569}{0.2500} = \mathbf{0.04823 \text{ M}}$ (4 sig figs).

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates Molar Mass including hydration water ($126.068 \text{ g/mol}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates moles of the standard.
  • 3 Marks: Correct final molarity calculation.

Question 3: Error Analysis - Spillage (Hard)

[2 Marks]

During step 3 of the preparation procedure, a student spills a few drops of the dissolved primary standard onto the bench while pouring it into the volumetric flask via the funnel. They continue with the procedure and make the flask up to the mark with water. Explain the effect this will have on the calculated concentration of the standard solution.

Hint: Think about the formula $c = n/V$. What happened to the true number of moles ($n$) that actually made it into the flask? What happened to the final volume ($V$)?

Worked Solution:

The true concentration of the solution will be lower than expected.

By spilling the dissolved standard, the number of moles ($n$) that actually enter the volumetric flask is less than what was originally weighed out. However, the student still fills the flask to the calibration mark, meaning the final volume ($V$) remains the same. According to $c = n/V$, a smaller numerator over a constant denominator results in a lower actual concentration.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly states the concentration will be lower.
  • 2 Marks: Provides a logical justification relating the loss of solute moles to the constant final volume.

Question 4: Purity Calculation (Extension)

[4 Marks]

A $5.00 \text{ g}$ sample of Sodium Carbonate is used to make a $250.0 \text{ mL}$ standard solution. Later analysis reveals the solution actually has a concentration of $0.180 \text{ M}$. Calculate the percentage purity of the original $5.00 \text{ g}$ solid sample.

Hint: Use the actual concentration ($0.180 \text{ M}$) and the volume to find the actual moles of pure $Na_2CO_3$ present. Convert that to mass. This is the mass of the pure chemical. Divide this by the $5.00 \text{ g}$ sample mass and multiply by 100.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Calculate actual moles of pure $Na_2CO_3$ present.
$n = c \times V = 0.180 \times 0.2500 = 0.0450 \text{ mol}$

Step 2: Calculate actual mass of pure $Na_2CO_3$.
$MM(Na_2CO_3) = 105.99 \text{ g/mol}$
$m = n \times MM = 0.0450 \times 105.99 = 4.76955 \text{ g}$

Step 3: Calculate percentage purity.
$\% \text{ Purity} = \frac{\text{Pure Mass}}{\text{Impure Sample Mass}} \times 100$
$\% \text{ Purity} = \frac{4.76955}{5.00} \times 100 = \mathbf{95.4\%}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly uses the actual concentration to find actual moles present.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly determines the Molar Mass of $Na_2CO_3$.
  • 3 Marks: Converts actual moles to actual mass of the pure substance.
  • 4 Marks: Correctly calculates the percentage purity ($95.4\%$).
