21. Boyle's Law

The Mathematical Relationship Between Pressure and Volume

The Compressibility of Gases

In the previous lesson, we established via the Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) that gases are mostly empty space, making them highly compressible. Boyle's Law, formulated by chemist Robert Boyle in the 1600s, mathematically defines exactly how a gas behaves when compressed.

Boyle's Law Definition

For a fixed mass of gas (constant moles, $n$) held at a strictly constant temperature ($T$), the pressure ($P$) of the gas is inversely proportional to its volume ($V$).

If you halve the volume of a container, the gas particles are squished into half the space. Because they are moving at the same speed (constant temperature), they will strike the walls of the container twice as often. Twice as many collisions means twice the pressure.

Mathematical Derivation

Since Pressure ($P$) is inversely proportional to Volume ($V$), we can write this relationship as:

$$ P \propto \frac{1}{V} $$

By introducing a mathematical constant of proportionality ($k$), we convert this into an equation:

$$ P = \frac{k}{V} \quad \implies \quad PV = k $$

This means that for any specific gas at a constant temperature, multiplying its Pressure by its Volume will always yield the same constant number ($k$). Therefore, if we change the state of the gas from State 1 to State 2, the product of $P$ and $V$ must remain identical.

$$ P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 $$

The Future Link: The Ideal Gas Law

You might be wondering, "What exactly is this mysterious constant $k$?"

While we are currently exploring the individual gas laws (Boyle's, Charles', etc.) one by one, you are actually being led toward the master equation of all gases: The Ideal Gas Law ($PV = nRT$).

If we look closely at $PV = nRT$:

  • $n$ is the number of moles (which is kept constant in Boyle's Law).
  • $T$ is the Temperature (which is kept constant in Boyle's Law).
  • $R$ is the Universal Gas Constant. On your NESA Reference Sheet, this is given precisely as $8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1}$.

Because $n$, $R$, and $T$ are all constants in Boyle's scenario, we can group them together: $nRT = k$. Hence, $PV = k$. By understanding this now, Phase E will come together seamlessly!

Worked Solutions

Study these exemplary worked solutions. Note that in Boyle's Law ($P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$), the units of Pressure and Volume do not need to be in standard SI units (like Kelvin for temperature), provided they are identical on both sides of the equation.

Easy

Example 1: Direct Substitution

A sealed syringe contains $50.0 \text{ mL}$ of air at a pressure of $1.00 \text{ atm}$. The plunger is pushed down, compressing the gas to a volume of $20.0 \text{ mL}$. Assuming the temperature remains constant, calculate the new pressure inside the syringe.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Extract the data
$P_1 = 1.00 \text{ atm}$
$V_1 = 50.0 \text{ mL}$
$P_2 = \text{ ?}$
$V_2 = 20.0 \text{ mL}$

Step 2: Apply Boyle's Law
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 1.00 \times 50.0 = P_2 \times 20.0 $$ $$ 50.0 = P_2 \times 20.0 $$

Step 3: Rearrange and solve
$$ P_2 = \frac{50.0}{20.0} $$ $$ P_2 = 2.50 \text{ atm} $$

Final Answer: The new pressure is $\mathbf{2.50 \text{ atm}}$.

Medium

Example 2: Finding Volume with Unit Conversion

A gas occupies a volume of $4.50 \text{ L}$ at a pressure of $105 \text{ kPa}$. The gas is allowed to expand until the pressure drops to standard atmospheric pressure ($1.00 \text{ atm}$). Calculate the final volume of the gas in Litres.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Unify the pressure units
We have $P_1$ in $kPa$ and $P_2$ in $atm$. They must match. Let's convert $P_2$ to $kPa$ using $1 \text{ atm} = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$.
$P_2 = 1.00 \times 101.3 = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$.

Step 2: Extract the data
$P_1 = 105 \text{ kPa}$
$V_1 = 4.50 \text{ L}$
$P_2 = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$
$V_2 = \text{ ?}$

Step 3: Apply the formula and solve
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 105 \times 4.50 = 101.3 \times V_2 $$ $$ 472.5 = 101.3 \times V_2 $$ $$ V_2 = \frac{472.5}{101.3} = 4.664... \text{ L} $$

Final Answer: The final volume is $\mathbf{4.66 \text{ L}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Proportional Changes

An industrial compressor reduces the volume of a gas chamber by exactly $40.0\%$. If the initial pressure inside the chamber was $150 \text{ kPa}$, calculate the final pressure inside the chamber. Assume constant temperature.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Establish the Volume Relationship
Let the initial volume be $V_1$.
If the volume is reduced by $40.0\%$, then the remaining volume is $60.0\%$ of the original.
Therefore, $V_2 = 0.600 \times V_1$.

Step 2: Apply Boyle's Law
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ Substitute $P_1 = 150 \text{ kPa}$ and $V_2 = 0.600V_1$:
$$ 150 \times V_1 = P_2 \times (0.600V_1) $$

Step 3: Solve algebraically
The $V_1$ terms on both sides of the equation cancel each other out.
$$ 150 = P_2 \times 0.600 $$ $$ P_2 = \frac{150}{0.600} = 250 \text{ kPa} $$

Final Answer: The new pressure is $\mathbf{250 \text{ kPa}}$.

Extension

Example 4: The Connected Flasks

Flask A has a volume of $2.00 \text{ L}$ and contains Oxygen gas at $300 \text{ kPa}$. Flask B has a volume of $3.00 \text{ L}$ and is a perfect vacuum ($0 \text{ kPa}$). The two flasks are connected by a closed valve. When the valve is opened, the oxygen gas expands to fill both flasks. Assuming constant temperature, calculate the final equilibrium pressure of the system.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Determine the Initial State
The gas only occupies Flask A initially.
$P_1 = 300 \text{ kPa}$
$V_1 = 2.00 \text{ L}$

Step 2: Determine the Final State
When the valve opens, the gas expands to fill the total available volume.
$V_2 = \text{Volume of Flask A} + \text{Volume of Flask B}$
$V_2 = 2.00 \text{ L} + 3.00 \text{ L} = 5.00 \text{ L}$
$P_2 = \text{ ?}$

Step 3: Apply Boyle's Law
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 300 \times 2.00 = P_2 \times 5.00 $$ $$ 600 = P_2 \times 5.00 $$ $$ P_2 = \frac{600}{5.00} = 120 \text{ kPa} $$

Final Answer: The final equilibrium pressure across both flasks is $\mathbf{120 \text{ kPa}}$.

Question 1: Direct Calculation (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A balloon contains $2.50 \text{ L}$ of helium gas at a pressure of $101.3 \text{ kPa}$. If the balloon is squeezed such that its volume decreases to $1.25 \text{ L}$, calculate the new pressure inside the balloon. Assume constant temperature.

Hint: Use $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$. Substitute $P_1 = 101.3$, $V_1 = 2.50$, and $V_2 = 1.25$. Rearrange to solve for $P_2$. Notice the volume halved!

Worked Solution:

$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$

$$ 101.3 \times 2.50 = P_2 \times 1.25 $$

$$ 253.25 = P_2 \times 1.25 $$

$$ P_2 = \frac{253.25}{1.25} = \mathbf{202.6 \text{ kPa}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes variables into the Boyle's Law formula ($101.3 \times 2.50 = P_2 \times 1.25$).
  • 2 Marks: Correct final answer of $202.6 \text{ kPa}$ (or $203 \text{ kPa}$ to 3 sig figs).

Question 2: Mismatched Units (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A gas occupies a volume of $800 \text{ mL}$ at a pressure of $1.50 \text{ atm}$. The container expands to a new volume of $2.40 \text{ L}$ at constant temperature. Calculate the new pressure in atmospheres.

Hint: The initial volume is in mL and the final volume is in L. They must be in the same unit before you use the formula. Convert $800 \text{ mL}$ to Litres first.

Worked Solution:

1. Unify Volume Units:
$V_1 = 800 \text{ mL} = 0.800 \text{ L}$

2. Apply Boyle's Law:
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 1.50 \times 0.800 = P_2 \times 2.40 $$ $$ 1.20 = P_2 \times 2.40 $$

3. Solve:
$$ P_2 = \frac{1.20}{2.40} = \mathbf{0.500 \text{ atm}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Recognises the unit mismatch and correctly converts volume so both match (e.g., $V_1 = 0.8 \text{ L}$ or $V_2 = 2400 \text{ mL}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly sets up the mathematical equation.
  • 3 Marks: Correct final pressure ($0.500 \text{ atm}$).

Question 3: Percentage Increase (Hard)

[3 Marks]

A gas is held in a flexible container at standard pressure ($101.3 \text{ kPa}$). The container is slowly stretched, increasing its volume by exactly $25.0\%$. Assuming the temperature remains constant, calculate the new pressure of the gas.

Hint: If the volume is increased *by* $25\%$, then the new volume ($V_2$) is $125\%$ of the original volume ($V_1$). Express this as $V_2 = 1.25 V_1$. Plug this into the formula; the $V_1$ will cancel out!

Worked Solution:

1. Establish Volume Ratio:
$V_2 = V_1 + 0.25V_1 = 1.25 V_1$

2. Apply Formula:
$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 101.3 \times V_1 = P_2 \times (1.25 V_1) $$

3. Solve:
Cancel $V_1$ from both sides:
$$ 101.3 = P_2 \times 1.25 $$ $$ P_2 = \frac{101.3}{1.25} = \mathbf{81.04 \text{ kPa}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly interprets the $25\%$ increase as a ratio (e.g., $V_2 = 1.25 V_1$).
  • 2 Marks: Substitutes the ratio into Boyle's Law allowing volumes to cancel.
  • 3 Marks: Correct final pressure ($81.04 \text{ kPa}$ or $81.0 \text{ kPa}$).

Question 4: The Deep Sea Diver (Extension)

[4 Marks]

A scuba diver releases a small air bubble with a volume of $15.0 \text{ mL}$ at a depth of $40.0 \text{ m}$ in the ocean, where the total pressure is $5.00 \text{ atm}$. The bubble rises to the surface where the pressure is exactly $1.00 \text{ atm}$.

Assuming the temperature of the water remains perfectly constant as the bubble rises, calculate the final volume of the bubble as it reaches the surface. Furthermore, explain using KMT why the bubble expands.

Hint: The depth ($40.0 \text{ m}$) is extra contextual information and is not needed for the calculation, as the initial pressure ($5.00 \text{ atm}$) is already given. Calculate the new volume. For KMT: relate the decreased external pressure to the internal collisions of the gas particles.

Worked Solution:

Part 1: Calculation
$P_1 = 5.00 \text{ atm}$
$V_1 = 15.0 \text{ mL}$
$P_2 = 1.00 \text{ atm}$

$$ P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 $$ $$ 5.00 \times 15.0 = 1.00 \times V_2 $$ $$ 75.0 = 1.00 \times V_2 $$ $$ V_2 = \mathbf{75.0 \text{ mL}} $$

Part 2: KMT Explanation
According to KMT, the gas particles inside the bubble are constantly colliding with the bubble's boundary, creating an outward internal pressure. At depth, the immense external water pressure compresses the bubble until internal and external pressures balance. As the bubble rises, the external pressure decreases. Because the internal particles are still colliding with the same kinetic energy (constant $T$), their outward force overcomes the weakening external pressure, causing the bubble to expand. It expands until the increased volume reduces the internal collision rate enough to balance the new $1.00 \text{ atm}$ external pressure.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies the correct $P_1$, $V_1$, and $P_2$ values, ignoring the distractor depth data.
  • 2 Marks: Correctly calculates the final volume ($75.0 \text{ mL}$).
  • 3 Marks: KMT Explanation notes that decreasing external pressure allows the internal collisions to expand the boundary.
  • 4 Marks: Connects the expansion to a resulting decrease in internal collision frequency until it matches the $1.00 \text{ atm}$ external pressure.
