20. Gas Properties & KMT

Kinetic Molecular Theory and the Behaviour of Ideal Gases

The Invisible World of Gases

Unlike solids, which maintain a rigid shape, or liquids, which maintain a set volume, gases are entirely free to expand, compress, and fill whatever container they are placed in. Before we can perform mathematical calculations regarding gases (Phase E), we must thoroughly understand the physical rules that govern their behaviour.

Chemists use a theoretical model called the Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) to explain why gases behave the way they do. This theory provides a set of "rules" that describe an imaginary, perfect gas known as an Ideal Gas.

The 5 Postulates of Kinetic Molecular Theory

  1. Continuous Random Motion: Gas particles (atoms or molecules) are in constant, rapid, straight-line motion. They only change direction when they collide with something.
  2. Negligible Volume: The actual volume of the individual gas particles is considered effectively zero compared to the vast empty space between them in the container.
  3. Elastic Collisions: When particles collide with each other or the walls of the container, no kinetic energy is lost to friction or heat. The collisions are perfectly elastic.
  4. No Intermolecular Forces: Gas particles exert absolutely zero attractive or repulsive forces on one another. They behave entirely independently.
  5. Temperature is Kinetic Energy: The average kinetic energy of the gas particles is directly proportional to the absolute temperature (measured in Kelvin). Hotter gases move faster.

🎯 The Ultimate Destination

Why do we care about KMT? Because all of these "ideal" assumptions lead us mathematically to the most important equation in gas chemistry: The Ideal Gas Law ($PV = nRT$). By establishing these ground rules, we can predict exactly how a gas will respond if we heat it, compress it, or add more moles to the container. We will explore $PV=nRT$ in depth in Lesson 26.

The Language of Gas Variables

To mathematically manipulate gas laws, you must be absolutely fluent in converting between different units of measurement. In chemistry, failing to convert Celsius to Kelvin is the most frequent cause of lost marks.

Variable Standard Chemistry Unit Common Conversions
Temperature ($T$) Kelvin ($K$) $K = \text{°C} + 273.15$
(Note: You can never have a negative Kelvin value. $0\text{ K}$ is Absolute Zero).
Pressure ($P$) Kilopascals ($kPa$) or Atmospheres ($atm$) $1 \text{ atm} = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$
$1 \text{ atm} = 760 \text{ mmHg}$
Volume ($V$) Litres ($L$) $1 \text{ L} = 1000 \text{ mL}$
$1 \text{ m}^3 = 1000 \text{ L}$

Interactive: Ideal vs. Real Gases

KMT assumes that gas particles have zero volume and zero attractive forces (an Ideal Gas). In reality, all gases are Real Gases—their particles do take up a tiny amount of space and they do slightly attract each other.

Most of the time, real gases behave ideally. However, under conditions of very high pressure (particles squished together) or very low temperature (particles moving too slowly to escape attraction), the ideal gas rules break down, and the gas condenses into a liquid.

[Image comparing ideal gas vs real gas behavior at low temperatures]

Ideal Gas Mode

High Temperature, Low Pressure. Particles ignore each other.

Real Gas Mode

Low Temp, High Pressure. Particles attract and clump (liquefy).

Worked Solutions

Study these examples to understand how to apply KMT concepts and unit conversions to solve problems.

Example 1: The Absolute Temperature Conversion (Easy)

A gas cylinder is stored in a freezer at $-45.0 \text{ °C}$. Calculate the absolute temperature of the gas in Kelvin, and explain why Kelvin must be used in gas law calculations according to KMT.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert to Kelvin
$$T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15$$ $$T(K) = -45.0 + 273.15 = 228.15 \text{ K}$$

Step 2: Explanation
According to the Kinetic Molecular Theory, the average kinetic energy of gas particles is directly proportional to absolute temperature. If we used Celsius, a temperature of $0 \text{ °C}$ or a negative value would imply zero or negative kinetic energy (and negative volumes/pressures in calculations), which is physically impossible. The Kelvin scale starts at Absolute Zero ($0 \text{ K}$), where all particle motion theoretically stops.

Example 2: Pressure Unit Conversions (Medium)

A car tyre is inflated to a pressure of $245 \text{ kPa}$. Convert this pressure into atmospheres ($atm$) and millimetres of mercury ($mmHg$).

Worked Solution:

Part A: Converting to atm
Conversion factor: $1 \text{ atm} = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$
$$P(atm) = \frac{245 \text{ kPa}}{101.3 \text{ kPa/atm}} = \mathbf{2.42 \text{ atm}}$$

Part B: Converting to mmHg
Conversion factor: $1 \text{ atm} = 760 \text{ mmHg}$
$$P(mmHg) = 2.418... \text{ atm} \times 760 \text{ mmHg/atm} = \mathbf{1840 \text{ mmHg}}$$

Example 3: Explaining Macroscopic Properties using KMT (Hard)

Using the postulates of the Kinetic Molecular Theory, explain why increasing the temperature of a gas inside a rigid, sealed container causes the pressure of the gas to increase.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Relate Temperature to Kinetic Energy
According to KMT, temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. Increasing the temperature causes the gas particles to move with a higher average velocity.

Step 2: Relate Velocity to Collisions
Because the container is rigid (volume is constant), the faster-moving particles will strike the walls of the container more frequently (higher collision rate) and with greater force.

Step 3: Define Pressure
Pressure is defined as the force exerted by particles colliding per unit area of the container walls. Since the collisions are more frequent and more forceful, the overall pressure of the gas increases.

Example 4: Deviations from Ideal Behaviour (Extension)

Ammonia gas ($NH_3$) and Helium gas ($He$) are placed in separate containers under extremely high pressure and low temperature. Explain which gas will deviate most significantly from ideal gas behaviour, referencing specific KMT postulates.

Worked Solution:

Answer: Ammonia ($NH_3$) will deviate most significantly from ideal behaviour.

Justification:
The Kinetic Molecular Theory postulates that ideal gas particles experience negligible intermolecular forces of attraction.

At high pressures, particles are forced very close together. At low temperatures, they possess low kinetic energy, meaning they cannot easily overcome attractive forces when they pass each other.

Ammonia is a highly polar molecule capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds with other ammonia molecules. Helium, conversely, is a noble gas and exhibits only extremely weak dispersion forces. Therefore, under conditions that favour condensation (high $P$, low $T$), the strong intermolecular forces between $NH_3$ molecules will cause them to attract and clump together, severely violating the KMT assumption of zero attractive forces. Helium will behave much closer to an ideal gas.

Question 1: Essential Unit Conversions (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A chemistry experiment requires data to be in standard SI/gas law units. Convert a temperature of $25.0 \text{ °C}$ to Kelvin, and a volume of $450 \text{ mL}$ to Litres.

Hint: Add $273.15$ to Celsius to get Kelvin. Divide millilitres by $1000$ to get Litres.

Worked Solution:

Temperature: $T(K) = 25.0 + 273.15 = \mathbf{298.15 \text{ K}}$

Volume: $V(L) = \frac{450}{1000} = \mathbf{0.450 \text{ L}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts temperature to $298.15 \text{ K}$ (or $298 \text{ K}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly converts volume to $0.450 \text{ L}$.

Question 2: Analysing Pressure Data (Medium)

[2 Marks]

During a reaction, a pressure sensor records a reading of $0.850 \text{ atm}$. Convert this reading into kilopascals ($kPa$). Provide your answer to 3 significant figures.

Hint: The conversion factor is $1 \text{ atm} = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$. Multiply your atm value by this factor.

Worked Solution:

$$P(kPa) = 0.850 \text{ atm} \times 101.3 \text{ kPa/atm}$$

$$P(kPa) = 86.105 \text{ kPa}$$

Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{86.1 \text{ kPa}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly sets up the mathematical conversion using $101.3$.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final answer of $86.1 \text{ kPa}$.

Question 3: KMT and Compressibility (Hard)

[3 Marks]

Unlike liquids, gases are highly compressible. Outline the specific postulate(s) of the Kinetic Molecular Theory that account for this macroscopic property.

Hint: Think about the distance between particles. What does KMT say about the actual volume of the gas molecules compared to the space they occupy?

Worked Solution:

According to the Kinetic Molecular Theory, the actual physical volume of the individual gas particles is deemed negligible (essentially zero) compared to the vast amount of empty space between them in the container.

Because there is a large amount of empty space between particles, an external force can easily push the particles closer together, reducing the overall volume of the container. This explains why gases are highly compressible.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies that gas particles are separated by large distances / empty space.
  • 2 Marks: Explicitly references the KMT postulate stating that the volume of the particles themselves is negligible.
  • 3 Marks: Logically connects this empty space to the ability for external force to push particles closer together (compressibility).

Question 4: Real Gas Breakdown (Extension)

[4 Marks]

A researcher is studying the behaviour of Water Vapour ($H_2O$) inside a sealed piston. As the researcher drastically decreases the volume of the piston and cools the system, the measured pressure drops significantly below the value predicted by ideal gas calculations, and eventually droplets form on the glass.

Explain this phenomenon by detailing exactly how the assumptions of an "Ideal Gas" fail under these specific conditions.

Hint: Ideal gases assume NO intermolecular forces and NO volume. What happens to water vapour when it gets cold and compressed? Relate this back to the specific assumptions.

Worked Solution:

The KMT defines an Ideal Gas as having particles with zero intermolecular forces and zero physical volume. Water vapour under standard conditions behaves somewhat ideally, but fails under these extreme conditions for two reasons:

1. Intermolecular Forces: As temperature decreases, the kinetic energy of the $H_2O$ molecules decreases. They move slower and are less able to overcome attractive forces. Furthermore, compressing the volume forces the molecules physically closer together. Water is highly polar and forms strong hydrogen bonds. These attractive forces pull the molecules together, causing them to collide with the walls with less force, which explains the unexpected drop in measured pressure.

2. Condensation: The combination of high pressure (proximity) and low temperature (low kinetic energy) allows the hydrogen bonds to fully lock the molecules together, transitioning the state from a gas to a liquid (condensation). The assumption that particles act independently has completely failed.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Identifies that KMT assumes zero intermolecular forces.
  • 2 Marks: Explains that low temperature (low KE) prevents particles from overcoming attractive forces.
  • 3 Marks: Explains that decreased volume forces particles closer together, exacerbating the effect of polarity/hydrogen bonding in water.
  • 4 Marks: Concludes that the attractive forces reduce wall collision impact (lowering pressure) and eventually cause the gas to condense.
