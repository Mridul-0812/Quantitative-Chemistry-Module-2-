12. Intro to Limiting Reagents

What "Runs Out First" Dictates the Yield

The "Real World" Chemistry Problem

Up until now, our calculations have assumed a perfect world. If you wanted to burn some methane, we just assumed you magically had exactly the right amount of oxygen. But in reality, when you mix chemicals in a laboratory, you rarely measure out the exact stoichiometric perfection of both reactants.

Usually, you just chuck a lump of metal into a massive beaker of acid. The metal completely dissolves and disappears, but there is still plenty of acid left over in the beaker. Because the metal ran out, the reaction stopped.

Terminology Alert!

Note: The words Reagent and Reactant mean the exact same thing and are used interchangeably in exams.

Limiting Reagent

The reactant that is completely consumed first. Because it runs out, it limits the amount of product that can be formed. Once it is gone, the reaction stops entirely.

Excess Reagent

The reactant(s) that are left over after the reaction has stopped. You had more than you needed to react with the limiting reagent.

🌭 The Hot Dog Analogy

Imagine you are hosting a BBQ. To make one perfect Hot Dog, your recipe is:

1 Sausage + 1 Bun + 2 Squirts of Mustard $\rightarrow$ 1 Hot Dog

You look in your pantry and find your inventory:

  • 10 Sausages
  • 8 Buns
  • 50 Squirts of Mustard

How many complete Hot Dogs can you make? You can only make 8. Why? Because you run out of Buns first! The Buns limit the reaction. It does not matter that you have enough mustard to feed an army; once the Buns are gone, production stops. Buns are the Limiting Reagent. Sausages and Mustard are in Excess.

How to find the Limiting Reagent Mathematically

In chemistry, you cannot just look at the grams, or even the moles, to immediately see what runs out first. Why? Because reactants are consumed at different rates depending on their coefficients in the balanced equation.

If the equation is $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3$, the Hydrogen ($H_2$) is being consumed three times faster than the Nitrogen ($N_2$). If you have 2 moles of each, the Hydrogen will still run out first because it's being used up rapidly.

The Limiting Reagent Test

For a general reaction: $\mathbf{aA + bB \rightarrow cC}$

Test Value = $\frac{\text{Actual Moles } (n)}{\text{Coefficient in Equation}}$
  1. Calculate this test value for ALL reactants.
  2. The reactant with the SMALLEST test value is the Limiting Reagent.

⚠️ Critical Rule!

The "Test Value" is purely for comparison to find the winner/loser. It is a fake number. Do not use the test value in any further stoichiometry calculations! Once you have identified the limiting reagent, go back and use its actual starting moles to find your products.

Fundamental Reactions to Memorise

From this point onward, many practice questions and exams will simply state the reactants (e.g., "Calcium carbonate is added to nitric acid"). You are expected to know the products by heart, write the states, and balance it.

If a question involves one of these core reaction types, the products will no longer be provided to you.

Reaction Type General Word Equation Example
Acid + Reactive Metal Acid + Metal $\rightarrow$ Salt + Hydrogen Gas $2HCl(aq) + Mg(s) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)$
Acid + Base (Neutralisation) Acid + Base $\rightarrow$ Salt + Water $H_2SO_4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) \rightarrow Na_2SO_4(aq) + 2H_2O(l)$
Acid + Carbonate Acid + Carbonate $\rightarrow$ Salt + Water + Carbon Dioxide $2HNO_3(aq) + CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow Ca(NO_3)_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)$

Basic Worked Examples

In this lesson, we will focus purely on identifying the Limiting Reagent using moles. We will tackle the massive Grams $\rightarrow$ Grams Limiting Reagent problems in the next lesson.

Example 1: The Hot Dog Math (Easy)

Reaction: $1 \text{ Sausage} + 1 \text{ Bun} \rightarrow 1 \text{ Hot Dog}$
If you have $5 \text{ moles}$ of Sausages and $3 \text{ moles}$ of Buns, which is the limiting reagent?

Step 1: Run the Test ($\frac{n}{\text{coeff}}$)

Test for Sausages: $\frac{5}{1} = 5$

Test for Buns: $\frac{3}{1} = 3$

Step 2: Compare

$3 < 5$. Therefore, the Buns have the smaller test value.

Answer: The Buns are the Limiting Reagent. The Sausages are in excess.

Example 2: Simple $1:1$ Ratio (Easy)

Carbon burns in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide: $C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g)$
If you mix $2.5 \text{ moles}$ of Carbon with $3.0 \text{ moles}$ of Oxygen gas, which is the limiting reagent?

Step 1: Check coefficients

The equation is balanced with $1:1:1$ coefficients.

Step 2: Run the Test ($\frac{n}{\text{coeff}}$)

Test for $C$: $\frac{2.5}{1} = 2.5$

Test for $O_2$: $\frac{3.0}{1} = 3.0$

Step 3: Conclusion

$2.5 < 3.0$. Carbon has the smaller test value.

Answer: Carbon ($C$) is the Limiting Reagent. Oxygen ($O_2$) is in excess.

Example 3: Different Coefficients (Medium)

Nitrogen gas and hydrogen gas react: $N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)$
A reaction vessel contains $4.0 \text{ moles}$ of Nitrogen and $9.0 \text{ moles}$ of Hydrogen. Determine the limiting reagent.

Step 1: Run the Test ($\frac{n}{\text{coeff}}$)

Test for $N_2$: $\frac{4.0}{1} = 4.0$

Test for $H_2$: $\frac{9.0}{3} = 3.0$

Step 2: Conclusion

Wait! Even though you have more physical moles of Hydrogen ($9.0 > 4.0$), Hydrogen is the limiting reagent because its test value ($3.0$) is smaller than Nitrogen's test value ($4.0$). It gets used up three times as fast!

Answer: Hydrogen ($H_2$) is the Limiting Reagent.

Practice Questions

Focus only on identifying the Limiting Reagent using the mathematical test.

Question 1

[2 Marks]

Equation: $2H_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2H_2O(g)$

You have $10.0 \text{ moles}$ of $H_2$ and $6.0 \text{ moles}$ of $O_2$. Show mathematical working to identify the limiting reagent.

Hint: Divide the moles of $H_2$ by 2. Divide the moles of $O_2$ by 1. Compare.

Worked Solution:

Test for $H_2$: $\frac{10.0}{2} = 5.0$

Test for $O_2$: $\frac{6.0}{1} = 6.0$

$5.0 < 6.0$, therefore $H_2$ has the smaller test value.

Limiting Reagent: $H_2$

  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates test values ($5.0$ and $6.0$).
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies $H_2$ as limiting.

Question 2

[2 Marks]

Solid aluminium reacts with chlorine gas: $2Al(s) + 3Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2AlCl_3(s)$

If $4.5 \text{ moles}$ of $Al$ and $6.0 \text{ moles}$ of $Cl_2$ are reacted, identify the limiting reagent.

Hint: Test $Al$ = $4.5 / 2$. Test $Cl_2$ = $6.0 / 3$.

Worked Solution:

Test for $Al$: $\frac{4.5}{2} = 2.25$

Test for $Cl_2$: $\frac{6.0}{3} = 2.00$

$2.00 < 2.25$.

Limiting Reagent: Chlorine gas ($Cl_2$)

  • 1 Mark: Correctly calculates test values ($2.25$ and $2.00$).
  • 1 Mark: Correctly identifies $Cl_2$ as limiting.

Question 3

[3 Marks]

Methane combusts in oxygen. (Refer to the memorisation table for the reaction).

If $2.5 \text{ moles}$ of methane is mixed with $4.0 \text{ moles}$ of oxygen gas and ignited, what is the limiting reagent?

Hint: You must write and balance the equation first! $CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$.

Worked Solution:

1. Equation: $CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(g)$

2. Test $CH_4$: $\frac{2.5}{1} = 2.5$

3. Test $O_2$: $\frac{4.0}{2} = 2.0$

$2.0 < 2.5$, so $O_2$ has the smaller test value.

Limiting Reagent: $O_2$

  • 1 Mark: Correct balanced chemical equation.
  • 1 Mark: Correct test values calculated.
  • 1 Mark: Correct identification of $O_2$.

Question 4 (Extension)

[3 Marks]

Using the situation from Question 3 (Methane and Oxygen), how many moles of $CO_2$ will actually be produced?

Hint: Use the actual moles of the LIMITING REAGENT ($4.0 \text{ moles}$ of $O_2$) and the stoichiometric ratio to find $CO_2$. Do NOT use the test value ($2.0$).

Worked Solution:

Limiting Reagent = $O_2$. Actual moles of $O_2 = 4.0 \text{ mol}$.

Equation: $CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow 1CO_2 + 2H_2O$

Ratio Want($CO_2$) / Have($O_2$) = $\frac{1}{2}$

$n(CO_2) = n(O_2) \times \frac{1}{2}$

$n(CO_2) = 4.0 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{2.0 \text{ moles}}$

  • 1 Mark: Selects the correct starting moles ($4.0 \text{ mol } O_2$) instead of methane or the test value.
  • 1 Mark: Applies correct stoichiometric ratio ($1/2$).
  • 1 Mark: Correct final answer ($2.0 \text{ moles}$).
