25. Combined Gas Law

The Mathematical Synthesis of Pressure, Volume, and Temperature

Bringing It All Together

In previous lessons, we examined the individual behaviour of gases by holding one variable strictly constant to observe the relationship between the other two. However, in the real world, conditions rarely cooperate so perfectly. Imagine a weather balloon rising through the atmosphere: as it ascends, the external pressure drops, but the atmospheric temperature drops simultaneously. Both volume and temperature are changing at the same time!

The Synthesis of Three Laws

We can merge the three individual gas laws into a single, comprehensive relationship. Let's trace the logic:

  • Boyle's Law: Pressure is inversely proportional to Volume ($P \propto \frac{1}{V}$ or $PV = k$).
  • Charles' Law: Volume is directly proportional to Temperature ($V \propto T$ or $\frac{V}{T} = k$).
  • Amontons' Law: Pressure is directly proportional to Temperature ($P \propto T$ or $\frac{P}{T} = k$).

When we combine these proportionalities, we find that the product of Pressure and Volume, divided by Temperature, yields a constant value for any fixed mass of gas.

The Combined Gas Law Equation

Because $\frac{PV}{T} = k$ (a constant), we can equate two different states of the exact same gas system. This gives us the incredibly powerful Combined Gas Law:

$$ \frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} $$

⚠️ Critical Temperature Rule

Gas laws are based on the Absolute Temperature Scale. You must convert all Celsius temperatures to Kelvin before calculating:

$$ T(\text{Kelvin}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15 $$

Note: $0 \ ^\circ\text{C} = 273.15 \text{ K}$. Failure to convert will result in mathematically incorrect ratios.

The Future Link: The Universal Gas Constant

You may be wondering why the expression $\frac{PV}{T}$ equals a constant ($k$). What is that constant?

As we transition toward Lesson 26 (The Ideal Gas Law), we can finally reveal what this constant is. If we take the master equation $PV = nRT$ and rearrange it to isolate $\frac{PV}{T}$, we get:

$$ \frac{PV}{T} = nR $$

Here, $n$ represents the number of moles of gas. Since the Combined Gas Law deals with a closed system (a sealed balloon, a piston), the number of moles ($n$) cannot change. The letter $R$ represents the Universal Gas Constant.

The Universal Gas Constant ($R$) is provided on your NESA Chemistry Reference Sheet. Its value is exactly:

$$ R = 8.314 \text{ J K}^{-1} \text{ mol}^{-1} $$

Because both $n$ and $R$ are completely locked constants in a sealed container, the term $nR$ evaluates to a single, unchanging number. This proves unequivocally why $\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1}$ must equal $\frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$!

Worked Solutions

Study these exemplary worked solutions. The key to mastering this equation is meticulous data extraction. Always list $P_1, V_1, T_1$ and $P_2, V_2, T_2$ on the side of your page before doing any algebra.

Easy

Example 1: Direct Substitution

A gas occupies a volume of $2.50 \text{ L}$ at a pressure of $1.50 \text{ atm}$ and a temperature of $300 \text{ K}$. If the volume is decreased to $1.20 \text{ L}$ and the temperature is raised to $400 \text{ K}$, calculate the new pressure of the gas.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Extract the data
$P_1 = 1.50 \text{ atm} \quad \quad P_2 = \text{ ?}$
$V_1 = 2.50 \text{ L} \quad \quad \quad V_2 = 1.20 \text{ L}$
$T_1 = 300 \text{ K} \quad \quad \quad T_2 = 400 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Apply the Combined Gas Law
$$ \frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{1.50 \times 2.50}{300} = \frac{P_2 \times 1.20}{400} $$

Step 3: Solve algebraically
Evaluate the left side first:
$$ \frac{3.75}{300} = \frac{1.20 \ P_2}{400} $$ $$ 0.0125 = \frac{1.20 \ P_2}{400} $$ Multiply by 400:
$$ 5.00 = 1.20 \ P_2 $$ $$ P_2 = \frac{5.00}{1.20} = 4.166... \text{ atm} $$

Final Answer: The new pressure is $\mathbf{4.17 \text{ atm}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Medium

Example 2: Mismatched Units & Celsius

A weather balloon is released from the ground where the pressure is $101.3 \text{ kPa}$, the temperature is $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$, and its volume is $4.00 \text{ L}$. It rises to an altitude where the pressure drops to $0.500 \text{ atm}$ and the temperature falls to $-15.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the final volume of the balloon in Litres.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Unify Units (Crucial!)
Convert both temperatures to Kelvin:
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$
$T_2 = -15.0 + 273.15 = 258.15 \text{ K}$

Unify the pressure units. Convert $P_2$ from atm to kPa ($1 \text{ atm} = 101.3 \text{ kPa}$):
$P_2 = 0.500 \times 101.3 = 50.65 \text{ kPa}$

Step 2: Extract data
$P_1 = 101.3 \text{ kPa} \quad \quad P_2 = 50.65 \text{ kPa}$
$V_1 = 4.00 \text{ L} \quad \quad \quad \ \ V_2 = \text{ ?}$
$T_1 = 298.15 \text{ K} \quad \quad \ T_2 = 258.15 \text{ K}$

Step 3: Solve
$$ \frac{101.3 \times 4.00}{298.15} = \frac{50.65 \times V_2}{258.15} $$ $$ 1.359... = \frac{50.65 \ V_2}{258.15} $$ $$ 350.84... = 50.65 \ V_2 $$ $$ V_2 = 6.926... \text{ L} $$

Final Answer: The new volume is $\mathbf{6.93 \text{ L}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Hard

Example 3: Solving for the Denominator ($T_2$)

A gas system initially sits at $120 \text{ kPa}$, $5.00 \text{ L}$, and $20.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. The gas is compressed to $2.50 \text{ L}$ and the pressure spikes to $300 \text{ kPa}$. Calculate the final temperature of the gas in degrees Celsius.

Worked Solution:

Step 1: Convert Initial Temp to Kelvin
$T_1 = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Setup Equation
$$ \frac{120 \times 5.00}{293.15} = \frac{300 \times 2.50}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{600}{293.15} = \frac{750}{T_2} $$

Step 3: Solve for $T_2$ via Cross-Multiplication
$$ 600 \times T_2 = 750 \times 293.15 $$ $$ 600 \times T_2 = 219862.5 $$ $$ T_2 = 366.4375 \text{ K} $$

Step 4: Convert back to Celsius
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 366.4375 - 273.15 = 93.2875 \ ^\circ\text{C} $$

Final Answer: The final temperature is $\mathbf{93.3 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$ (to 3 sig figs).

Extension

Example 4: Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)

A student collects $45.0 \text{ mL}$ of Hydrogen gas over water in a lab that is $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ and has an atmospheric pressure of $105 \text{ kPa}$. In chemistry, Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP) is strictly defined as $0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ ($273.15 \text{ K}$) and $100 \text{ kPa}$.

Calculate what the exact volume of this gas sample would be if it were mathematically moved to STP conditions.

Worked Solution:

In the HSC, calculating a gas volume at STP from laboratory conditions is a highly common requirement before calculating moles.

Step 1: Define State 1 (Lab Conditions)
$P_1 = 105 \text{ kPa}$
$V_1 = 45.0 \text{ mL}$
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$

Step 2: Define State 2 (STP Conditions)
$P_2 = 100 \text{ kPa}$ (Standard Pressure)
$T_2 = 273.15 \text{ K}$ (Standard Temperature)
$V_2 = \text{ ?}$

Step 3: Solve using Combined Gas Law
$$ \frac{105 \times 45.0}{298.15} = \frac{100 \times V_2}{273.15} $$ $$ 15.8477... = \frac{100 \ V_2}{273.15} $$ Multiply by 273.15:
$$ 4328.79... = 100 \ V_2 $$ $$ V_2 = 43.287... \text{ mL} $$

Final Answer: At STP, the gas would occupy a volume of $\mathbf{43.3 \text{ mL}}$.

Practise Questions

Apply your understanding of the Combined Gas Law. Write your working out on paper, ensure you check your units, reveal the solutions, and self-assess using the marking criteria.

Question 1: Direct Substitution (Easy)

[2 Marks]

A gas has a volume of $8.00 \text{ L}$ at a pressure of $2.00 \text{ atm}$ and a temperature of $400 \text{ K}$. Calculate the final volume if the pressure changes to $1.50 \text{ atm}$ and the temperature decreases to $300 \text{ K}$.

Hint: The temperature is already given in Kelvin, and the pressure units match. Plug $P_1=2.00, V_1=8.00, T_1=400$ and $P_2=1.50, T_2=300$ into the formula and solve for $V_2$.

Worked Solution:

$$ \frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} $$

$$ \frac{2.00 \times 8.00}{400} = \frac{1.50 \times V_2}{300} $$

$$ \frac{16.00}{400} = \frac{1.50 \ V_2}{300} $$

$$ 0.0400 = \frac{1.50 \ V_2}{300} $$

$$ 12.0 = 1.50 \ V_2 $$

$$ V_2 = \mathbf{8.00 \text{ L}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly substitutes all variables into the Combined Gas Law formula.
  • 2 Marks: Correct final volume of $8.00 \text{ L}$.

Question 2: Unit Conversions (Medium)

[3 Marks]

A $250 \text{ mL}$ flask contains Neon gas at $25.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $95.0 \text{ kPa}$. The gas is transferred to a $1.50 \text{ L}$ container and heated to $80.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the new pressure of the gas in $kPa$.

Hint: Watch out for the traps! Temperatures must be in Kelvin ($+273.15$). Volumes must be in the same unit (convert $250 \text{ mL}$ to $0.250 \text{ L}$). Then solve for $P_2$.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert Units:
$T_1 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$
$T_2 = 80.0 + 273.15 = 353.15 \text{ K}$
$V_1 = 250 \text{ mL} = 0.250 \text{ L}$ (to match $V_2$ which is $1.50 \text{ L}$)

2. Apply Formula:
$$ \frac{95.0 \times 0.250}{298.15} = \frac{P_2 \times 1.50}{353.15} $$

3. Solve for $P_2$:
$$ 0.07965... = \frac{1.50 \ P_2}{353.15} $$ $$ 28.130... = 1.50 \ P_2 $$ $$ P_2 = \mathbf{18.75... \text{ kPa}} $$ Rounded to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{18.8 \text{ kPa}}$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts both temperatures to Kelvin.
  • 2 Mark: Correctly standardises volume units (both to mL or both to L).
  • 3 Marks: Correct final pressure ($\approx 18.8 \text{ kPa}$).

Question 3: Solving for Final Temperature (Hard)

[4 Marks]

A gas sample initially at $1.20 \text{ atm}$ and $10.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ occupies $15.0 \text{ L}$. The gas is compressed into a $5.00 \text{ L}$ tank, causing the internal pressure to spike to $4.50 \text{ atm}$. Calculate the final temperature of the gas in degrees Celsius.

Hint: Step 1: Convert $10.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin. Step 2: Use the formula to find $T_2$ in Kelvin. Cross-multiplication is the safest algebra method here. Step 3: Convert the final answer back to Celsius.

Worked Solution:

1. Convert $T_1$ to Kelvin:
$T_1 = 10.0 + 273.15 = 283.15 \text{ K}$

2. Apply Formula:
$$ \frac{1.20 \times 15.0}{283.15} = \frac{4.50 \times 5.00}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{18.0}{283.15} = \frac{22.5}{T_2} $$

3. Cross-multiply and solve:
$$ 18.0 \times T_2 = 22.5 \times 283.15 $$ $$ 18.0 \times T_2 = 6370.875 $$ $$ T_2 = 353.9375 \text{ K} $$

4. Convert back to Celsius:
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 353.9375 - 273.15 = \mathbf{80.7875 \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$ Rounding to 3 sig figs: $\mathbf{80.8 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$.

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Converts $T_1$ to $283.15 \text{ K}$.
  • 2 Marks: Correct algebraic setup for the fraction.
  • 3 Marks: Accurately isolates $T_2$ to find $\approx 353.9 \text{ K}$.
  • 4 Marks: Correctly converts the final temperature to Celsius ($80.8 \ ^\circ\text{C}$).

Question 4: Relative Percentages (Extension)

[4 Marks]

An engineer designs an engine cylinder. During the compression stroke, the volume of the gas is reduced to exactly one-third ($1/3$) of its original volume. Simultaneously, the pressure of the gas increases to exactly five times ($5\times$) its original pressure.

If the starting temperature of the gas was $27.0 \ ^\circ\text{C}$, prove mathematically what the final temperature must be in degrees Celsius.

Hint: You do not need numbers for P or V. Let the initial states be $P_1$ and $V_1$. Express the final states as $P_2 = 5 P_1$ and $V_2 = (1/3)V_1$. Put these directly into the Combined Gas Law equation and the $P_1$ and $V_1$ will cancel out entirely!

Worked Solution:

1. Define variables:
$T_1 = 27.0 + 273.15 = 300.15 \text{ K}$
$V_2 = \frac{1}{3} V_1$
$P_2 = 5 P_1$

2. Substitute into formula:
$$ \frac{P_1 V_1}{300.15} = \frac{(5 P_1) \times (\frac{1}{3} V_1)}{T_2} $$

3. Simplify and cancel:
Divide both sides by $(P_1 V_1)$:
$$ \frac{1}{300.15} = \frac{5 \times \frac{1}{3}}{T_2} $$ $$ \frac{1}{300.15} = \frac{\frac{5}{3}}{T_2} $$

4. Solve for $T_2$:
$$ T_2 = 300.15 \times \left(\frac{5}{3}\right) $$ $$ T_2 = 500.25 \text{ K} $$

5. Convert to Celsius:
$$ T(^\circ\text{C}) = 500.25 - 273.15 = \mathbf{227.1 \ ^\circ\text{C}} $$

Marking Criteria:
  • 1 Mark: Correctly converts $T_1$ to Kelvin ($300.15 \text{ K}$).
  • 2 Marks: Correctly defines the ratios $P_2 = 5P_1$ and $V_2 = (1/3)V_1$.
  • 3 Marks: Successfully cancels algebraic terms to find $T_2$ ($500.25 \text{ K}$).
  • 4 Marks: Correctly converts the final temperature back to Celsius ($227.1 \ ^\circ\text{C}$).
